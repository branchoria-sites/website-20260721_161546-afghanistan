## Introduction

[Antigua and Barbuda]({{ 'antigua-and-barbuda/' | relative_url }}) does not have a dedicated government office for investigating unidentified flying objects (UFOs) or unidentified anomalous phenomena (UAP). Instead, any unusual object observed in Antiguan airspace that could affect aviation safety would be handled through the country's normal civil aviation reporting system. In practice, this means reports flow through air traffic control, pilots, airport operators, airlines and the regional civil aviation authority rather than to a specialist UFO bureau.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Antigua_and_Barbuda_0f8518_antigua_airspace_rep_2c46aa-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Reporting System illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Antigua_and_Barbuda_0f8518_antigua_airspace_rep_2c46aa-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Antigua_and_Barbuda_0f8518_antigua_airspace_rep_2c46aa-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

This distinction is important. Aviation authorities are concerned with whether an unidentified object presents a hazard to aircraft, not with determining whether it is extraordinary. If an object cannot immediately be identified but creates a potential safety issue, it is recorded as an aviation occurrence and investigated using established safety procedures. Only if evidence remained unexplained after normal technical analysis would it continue to be regarded simply as an unidentified observation.

## National aviation responsibilities

Antigua and Barbuda is one of the member states served by the Eastern Caribbean Civil Aviation Authority (ECCAA). Rather than maintaining a separate national UFO reporting office, the country relies on ECCAA and its aviation safety framework for incidents affecting civil aviation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.eccaa.aero/index.php?Itemid=90&amp;id=175&amp;option=com_content&amp;view=article" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eccaa.aero">[ECCAA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eccaa.aero</span><span class="citation-popover-title">AIG Contact Information</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AIG Contact Information...</span></span></span>

Within this system, responsibility is divided across several organisations:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Air traffic controllers** monitor controlled airspace and document unusual traffic or hazards reported by pilots or detected through surveillance systems.
* **Airport operators**, particularly at V. C. Bird International Airport, manage aerodrome safety and coordinate with air traffic services when unusual aerial activity could affect arrivals or departures.
* **Aircraft operators and flight crews** are responsible for reporting safety occurrences encountered during flight.
* **ECCAA** oversees aviation regulation across Antigua and Barbuda and neighbouring Organisation of Eastern Caribbean States (OECS) members, including accident investigation and safety oversight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.eccaa.aero/index.php?Itemid=90&amp;id=175&amp;option=com_content&amp;view=article" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eccaa.aero">[ECCAA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eccaa.aero</span><span class="citation-popover-title">AIG Contact Information</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AIG Contact Information...</span></span></span>

</div>

None of these bodies advertises a separate category for "UFO investigations". Instead, unidentified objects are treated according to their operational significance.

## How pilots and controllers report unusual aerial hazards

For pilots, the critical question is not whether an object is mysterious but whether it poses a collision risk or interferes with safe flight.

If a crew observes an unidentified light, object or aircraft, the normal sequence is typically:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Report the sighting immediately to air traffic control.
2. Air traffic control checks radar, flight plans, surveillance systems and nearby traffic.
3. Other aircraft in the vicinity may be asked whether they observe the same object.
4. If the event represents a reportable safety occurrence, it is documented through the aviation occurrence reporting process.
5. If necessary, investigators review radar recordings, communications, meteorological information and any available photographs or video.

</div>

This approach follows the international safety philosophy promoted through the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO): unusual events are treated first as operational safety matters rather than as unexplained phenomena. Modern occurrence reporting systems exist primarily to identify hazards, improve procedures and prevent future accidents rather than assign blame or speculate about causes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.caa.co.uk/about-us/make-a-report-or-complaint/report-something/mor/occurrence-reporting/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: caa.co.uk">[Civil Aviation Authority]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">caa.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Civil Aviation Authority Occurrence reporting &#124; UK Civil Aviation Authority</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Civil Aviation Authority Occurrence reporting &#124; UK Civil Aviation Authority</span></span></span>

## Why there is no dedicated UFO desk

Unlike a small number of countries that have periodically operated military or scientific UFO projects, Antigua and Barbuda has no publicly known government office devoted to collecting civilian UFO reports.

Several practical factors explain this:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* The country's aviation administration is regional rather than national.
* Airspace management is already covered by established aviation safety systems.
* Most unusual aerial observations can usually be examined using conventional aviation tools such as radar records, aircraft tracking, satellite information and meteorological data.
* Public resources are focused on flight safety instead of maintaining a separate investigative programme for unidentified sightings.

</div>

As a result, a report describing an unexplained object would normally enter existing aviation reporting channels rather than a specialist UFO archive.

## What happens after a report?

Whether an event receives detailed investigation depends on its operational significance.

A brief public sighting with no effect on aircraft operations may never enter the formal aviation reporting system. By contrast, reports involving:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* possible collision risk,
* unexplained radar returns,
* interference with aircraft,
* airspace incursions,
* or multiple independent aviation witnesses,

</div>

are considerably more likely to generate official documentation and technical review.

If an event were sufficiently serious to constitute an aircraft accident or serious incident, ECCAA's Aircraft Accident Investigation function would become involved under international accident investigation procedures. These investigations seek factual explanations that improve aviation safety rather than determine whether an object represents an unknown phenomenon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.eccaa.aero/index.php?Itemid=90&amp;id=175&amp;option=com_content&amp;view=article" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eccaa.aero">[ECCAA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eccaa.aero</span><span class="citation-popover-title">AIG Contact Information</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AIG Contact Information...</span></span></span>

## Lessons from reported Antiguan sightings

The limited number of widely reported Antiguan aerial sightings illustrates how this system works in practice.

The better-known observations from December 2019 and March 2024 generated public discussion because residents saw unusual illuminated objects over the Caribbean. However, both episodes closely matched documented rocket launches whose high-altitude exhaust plumes and illuminated trajectories were visible across a broad region. Because conventional aerospace explanations were available, no public evidence indicates that either event led to a sustained aviation investigation into an unidentified object.

This reflects the normal pattern in aviation reporting: an observation may initially be unidentified, but once supported by launch schedules, satellite data or other evidence, it ceases to be an unresolved aviation occurrence.

## The reporting system in context

For readers exploring Antigua and Barbuda's broader record of unusual aerial sightings, the key point is that the country records aviation hazards through ordinary civil aviation governance rather than through a dedicated UFO agency.

An unidentified object affecting Antiguan airspace would therefore be documented—if at all—through the same professional reporting channels used for any other aviation safety occurrence. The emphasis is on protecting aircraft, verifying facts through technical evidence and determining whether the object can be identified using established aviation, meteorological and aerospace information before considering it genuinely unexplained.
