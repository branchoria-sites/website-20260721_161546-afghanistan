## Introduction

The reported lights seen from Mahé on the evening of 21 March 2020 remain one of the very few publicly indexed unidentified aerial phenomenon (UAP) reports from [Seychelles]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-seychelles-skies/' | relative_url }}). The available evidence, however, is extremely limited. The only detailed public account comes from a National UFO Reporting Center (NUFORC) witness report describing multiple point-like lights moving across the sky over roughly 90 minutes. Crucially, there are no publicly available photographs, videos, radar records, air traffic data or independent witness statements that allow the observation to be reconstructed with confidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/sighting/?id=154304" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[National UFO Reporting Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National UFO Reporting CenterNUFORC UFO Sighting 154304May 15, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 15, 2020</span></span></span>

Unlike reports describing a single structured object, this case is better understood as a question of competing ordinary explanations. The witness themselves even referenced "Starlink satellites" in the report title, reflecting the widespread public awareness of newly launched satellite constellations during early 2020. Whether the lights were satellites, aircraft, unrelated objects seen over an extended period, or something less easily explained cannot be determined from the available evidence.

## The reported movements across the sky

According to the NUFORC report, the observation began at approximately 7.00 p.m. local time on 21 March 2020 on Mahé. Two observers reportedly watched multiple lights over a period of around 90 minutes. The first lights were described as travelling from south to north. During the same observing period, other lights reportedly crossed west to east, south-west to north-east, and in apparently opposing directions. The report contains no description of solid objects, sound, coloured lights, abrupt acceleration or physical effects on the surroundings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/sighting/?id=154304" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[National UFO Reporting Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National UFO Reporting CenterNUFORC UFO Sighting 154304May 15, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 15, 2020</span></span></span>

## Why satellites became an immediate candidate

The timing of the report is notable. March 2020 fell during the rapid expansion of SpaceX's Starlink constellation, when newly launched satellite groups were generating an unprecedented number of public reports worldwide from people unfamiliar with seeing numerous bright satellites shortly after sunset. Astronomers and satellite observers were actively studying how these satellites appeared under different lighting conditions because their visibility was becoming a significant issue for both astronomy and casual skywatching.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07446" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Low Earth Orbit Satellite Population and Impacts of the SpaceX Starlink ConstellationMarch 16, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 16, 2020</span></span></span>

The witness's own reference to "Starlink satellites" indicates that satellites were already considered a possible explanation rather than being introduced later by investigators.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/sighting/?id=154304" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[National UFO Reporting Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National UFO Reporting CenterNUFORC UFO Sighting 154304May 15, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 15, 2020</span></span></span>

However, the description does not perfectly match the classic appearance of a newly launched Starlink "train". Those are typically seen as numerous satellites following one another along nearly the same orbital track. The Mahé report instead describes lights moving on several different apparent headings. That does not rule out satellites, because multiple unrelated satellites can be visible simultaneously in different parts of the sky, especially during evening twilight, but it does weaken the idea that a single Starlink train explains the entire observation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07446" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Low Earth Orbit Satellite Population and Impacts of the SpaceX Starlink ConstellationMarch 16, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 16, 2020</span></span></span>

## Satellites, aircraft, lanterns and other candidates

Several conventional explanations remain plausible, although none can be confirmed from the available evidence.

**Independent satellites**

This is arguably the strongest broad category. Around twilight, multiple satellites in different orbital planes may become visible at the same time. Their apparent directions vary depending on orbital inclination and the observer's position. Changes in brightness caused by reflective geometry can also make satellites appear unexpectedly bright or fade from view, contributing to unusual impressions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.07446" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Low Earth Orbit Satellite Population and Impacts of the SpaceX Starlink ConstellationMarch 16, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 16, 2020</span></span></span>

**Aircraft**

Aircraft following different routes could account for lights travelling on different headings. Without precise azimuths, elevation angles or descriptions of flashing navigation lights, it is impossible to compare the report against known air traffic. The report does not state whether the lights blinked, changed brightness gradually or maintained constant illumination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nuforc.org/sighting/?id=154304" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nuforc.org">[National UFO Reporting Center]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nuforc.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National UFO Reporting CenterNUFORC UFO Sighting 154304May 15, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 15, 2020</span></span></span>

**Chinese lanterns or drifting lights**

Lanterns usually drift with prevailing winds and therefore tend to share broadly similar directions. The report's description of multiple apparent headings makes lanterns a less straightforward fit, although separate launches at different times cannot be excluded. Because no information about wind conditions or object altitude is available, this explanation cannot be tested.

**Astronomical objects**

Bright planets and stars can create confusion when viewed intermittently through cloud, but they do not explain repeated movement across different headings over the reported period.

**An unidentified phenomenon**

The case cannot be eliminated entirely as unidentified because the observational record is simply too incomplete. The absence of explanatory data is not evidence for an extraordinary cause; it means the event cannot be classified with confidence.

## What tracking data would be needed

The Mahé report illustrates how difficult retrospective analysis becomes when basic observational information is missing.

To assess a satellite explanation properly, investigators would ideally need:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* the exact observing location;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.heavens-above.com/main.aspx/starlink.aspx" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: heavens-above.com">[heavens-above.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">heavens-above.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Get predictions here. ConfigurationLogin (optional)Change your observing locationSatellitesLive sky...</span></span></span>
* precise start and end times;
* compass bearings for each light;
* estimated elevation above the horizon;
* approximate angular speed;
* whether lights faded gradually or disappeared abruptly;
* photographs or video with timestamps.

</div>
