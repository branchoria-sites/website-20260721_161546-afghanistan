## Why the 1989–90 wave became exceptional

The decisive sequence began on 29 November 1989 around Eupen, close to Belgium’s borders with Germany and the Netherlands. Two gendarmes, Hubert von Montigny and Heinrich Nicoll, reported seeing an illuminated object that they interpreted as a large triangular platform. They followed or re-observed unusual lights over an extended period, while other police officers and civilians in the region submitted broadly similar accounts. SOBEPS-linked catalogues record roughly 150 notifications for that evening, although the precise number varies according to whether separate witnesses, locations or episodes are counted as individual cases.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cobeps.org/pdf/belgian_wave_130310.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cobeps.org">[cobeps.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cobeps.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Belgian Ufo wave</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">March 11, 2013 — On 29 November 1989, fourteen gendarmes of the Eupen region had observed a strange aerial phenomenon. Common descriptions included three bright lights arranged in a triangle, sometimes with a red or orange light near the centre; slow movement; hovering; and surprisingly little noise. Concentrations appeared around Eupen and Liège before spreading into Brabant, the Brussels area, Namur and communities around La Louvière. The numbers often quoted for the wave require caution. SOBEPS and successor researchers assembled hundreds of investigated reports and more than a thousand notifications across the broader 1989–93 period. One catalogue records 454 cases in 1989, 447 in 1990 and rapidly declining totals thereafter. These are reporting statistics, not counts of confirmed anomalous vehicles. Some entries were poorly documented, some involved several witnesses to the same stimulus, and some were later assigned ordinary explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cobeps.org/pdf/belgian_wave_130310.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cobeps.org">[cobeps.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cobeps.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Belgian Ufo wave</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">March 11, 2013 — On 29 November 1989, fourteen gendarmes of the Eupen region had observed a strange aerial phenomenon. Even so, the opening phase cannot be dismissed merely because the witnesses had heard UFO stories. Several police observations appear to have preceded the national media explosion, and the authorities treated at least some reports as possible aviation or security matters. The more difficult question is whether the witnesses accurately inferred a single solid triangle from lights seen at night. Human vision readily connects separate lights into a perceived outline, especially when distance, size and background references are missing.

## What happened during the F-16 interception

The most consequential incident occurred during the night of 30–31 March 1990. Reports reached the Control Reporting Centre at Glons of bright lights near Thorembais-Gembloux, south-east of Brussels. A gendarmerie patrol was asked to check the observation, and military controllers received intermittent radar indications. Two F-16 fighters were launched from Beauvechain Air Base.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgian_UFO_wave" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Belgian UFO wave</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Belgian UFO wave</span></span></span>

The scramble is an established fact, but many retellings exaggerate what it demonstrated. The fighters attempted several interceptions and recorded brief radar contacts, yet neither pilot visually observed a corresponding craft. The returns did not provide a continuous track of one object moving through Belgian airspace. Some apparent locks were associated with the other fighter, while sceptical technical analyses have attributed additional returns to propagation effects, interference or anomalous radar behaviour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgian_UFO_wave" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Belgian UFO wave</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Belgian UFO wave</span></span></span>

Claims that an object repeatedly accelerated from very low speed to extreme or supersonic velocity arise from treating short, unstable radar indications as a coherent physical trajectory. Without continuous tracking, independent range confirmation and visual correlation, successive returns cannot safely be assumed to represent the same target. The episode established that controllers and pilots encountered unexplained indications while responding to reports from the ground; it did not establish that a craft outran the F-16s.

The Belgian Air Force’s willingness to discuss the incident gave it greater credibility than most UFO stories. Colonel Wilfried De Brouwer, then responsible for Air Staff operations, publicly described the investigation and did not accuse the witnesses of fabrication. At the same time, the available military conclusion was limited: the Air Force had insufficient information to identify a physical object responsible for all the reports. Official attention should therefore be understood as evidence of uncertainty and responsible air-defence procedure, not as official confirmation of an extraterrestrial vehicle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vague_belge_d%27ovnis" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Vague belge d&#x27;ovnis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Vague belge d&#x27;ovnis</span></span></span>

## The evidence that survived scrutiny

The Belgian wave contains several kinds of evidence, and they should not be treated as interchangeable.

**Confirmed events include** the receipt of numerous reports, observations by serving police officers, exchanges between civilian investigators and military authorities, radar indications on 30–31 March, and the launch of two F-16s. These facts show that the wave was socially and operationally real.

**Contested evidence includes** witness estimates of enormous size, very low altitude, silence and extraordinary performance. Such details may be accurate in individual cases, but night observations without known distance make physical dimensions and speed exceptionally difficult to calculate. Similar descriptions across reports can reflect a common stimulus, but they can also be shaped by publicity, repeated questioning and the growing cultural template of the “Belgian triangle”.

**Debunked or substantially weakened evidence includes** several episodes with identifiable conventional causes. During one December 1989 alert in Limburg, pilots saw beams associated with lighting or a laser display reflecting from cloud. Other Belgian reports have been linked to aircraft, helicopters, astronomical objects, balloons, satellites and unusual atmospheric viewing conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vague_belge_d%27ovnis" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Vague belge d&#x27;ovnis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Vague belge d&#x27;ovnis</span></span></span>

This mixed record explains why sweeping verdicts are unhelpful. Saying that every witness invented a triangle goes beyond the evidence. Saying that hundreds of witnesses independently confirmed the same engineered craft also goes beyond it. The reports varied in quality, and the surviving documentation does not turn them into one scientifically verified event.

## How the famous photograph collapsed

The Petit-Rechain photograph became the wave’s defining image. It appeared to show three bright corner lights and a central light against a dark background, seemingly matching the triangular objects described by witnesses. Because there was no visible landscape, horizon or reliable scale, however, analysts could not determine the photographed object’s distance or size. The picture could represent something large and distant, or something small and close to the camera.

In July 2011, Patrick Maréchal said that he and others had fabricated the scene using a triangular piece of polystyrene fitted with lights and photographed it against the night sky. Reuters and Belgian media reported the confession, and COBEPS president Patrick Ferryn accepted that the image was fraudulent while arguing that one false photograph did not invalidate the wider wave.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/article/lifestyle/belgian-hit-ufo-image-was-polystyrene-says-forger-idUSTRE76Q2DE/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[reuters.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">belgian hit ufo image was polystyrene says forger id USTRE76Q2DE</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Belgian hit UFO image was polystyrene, says forger27 Jul 2011 — A Belgian UFO photograph that became a worldwide hit was faked wit...</span></span></span>

Later online claims have alleged that Maréchal withdrew or was paid to make his confession, but they have not supplied comparably strong, independently verified documentation. ## Competing explanations

No single conventional explanation accounts neatly for every Belgian report. The principal interpretations instead operate at different levels.

The **structured-craft interpretation** holds that at least some witnesses saw a real, unconventional vehicle. Its strongest points are the police testimony, the repeated triangular-light pattern and reports of slow, quiet movement. Its weakness is the lack of high-quality photographs, physical traces or a continuous multisensor track. The F-16 data do not supply that missing confirmation.

The **misidentified-aircraft interpretation** proposes that helicopters, aeroplanes or several lights viewed together generated many reports. Helicopters can hover and move slowly, while their sound may be weak or carried away from an observer by wind. Critics respond that some witnesses reported objects at apparently close range and remained convinced that no familiar aircraft matched what they saw. The hypothesis is plausible for part of the wave but cannot be demonstrated case by case without flight logs, precise geometry and weather data.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cobeps.org/pdf/ernage_report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cobeps.org">[cobeps.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cobeps.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ernage report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ernage report</span></span></span>

The **astronomical and atmospheric interpretation** is particularly relevant to reports consisting only of distant lights. Bright planets, stars seen through moving cloud, unusual refraction and searchlights projected onto low cloud can appear to change colour or move erratically. Such explanations become less persuasive where witnesses reported a sharply defined nearby structure, although those structure estimates themselves remain vulnerable to perceptual error.

The **psychosocial interpretation** treats the wave as an interaction between initial ambiguous sightings, intensive publicity and a recognisable cultural image. Once triangular UFOs dominated Belgian news, observers were more likely to notice unfamiliar lights, interpret them as triangles and report them. Sceptical researchers have argued that this process can produce clusters of sincere, mutually reinforcing testimony without mass dishonesty or hallucination. SOBEPS-associated researchers dispute that account, noting that some early reports were collected before the triangle had become a fully established media template.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.academia.edu/4321342/The_Beginning_of_the_Belgian_UFO_wave" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: academia.edu">[Academia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">academia.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">PDF) The Beginning of the Belgian UFO wave</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PDF) The Beginning of the Belgian UFO wave</span></span></span>

These mechanisms are not mutually exclusive. A credible reconstruction can include genuine but ordinary aircraft, astronomical objects, lighting displays, perceptual completion, social reinforcement, occasional faulty radar returns and deliberate hoaxes. The existence of unresolved cases means that the original stimulus cannot always be identified; it does not mean that the most extraordinary explanation becomes the default.

## Records, archives and reliability

Belgium’s UFO record is unusually extensive but institutionally fragmented. The most detailed case files were largely created by civilian organisations rather than by a permanent national investigative agency. SOBEPS interviews and field reports remain central to the historical record, while COBEPS maintains research material and the Catalogue of Belgian Observations. The catalogue applies basic requirements such as a reasonably known date and location, and it records weather, narrative details and assessments where available.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cobeps.org/pdf/belgian_wave_130310.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cobeps.org">[cobeps.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cobeps.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Belgian Ufo wave</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">March 11, 2013 — On 29 November 1989, fourteen gendarmes of the Eupen region had observed a strange aerial phenomenon. These [archives]({{ 'archives/' | relative_url }}) are valuable, especially where they preserve early testimony, but they are not neutral state databases. Investigators sometimes favoured unconventional interpretations, witness interviews were not always conducted under uniform protocols, and later publications mixed raw testimony with argument. Sceptical reconstructions have their own limitations when they propose an aircraft, helicopter or psychological explanation without enough case-specific data to prove it.

The Belgian Air Force material has greater official authority but much narrower scope. In UFO statistics, “unexplained” often means “insufficient evidence for identification”, rather than “evidence of an unknown technology”.

## What Belgium’s UFO history actually establishes

Belgium experienced an exceptional reporting wave, concentrated particularly in Wallonia and centred on the winter of 1989–90. Its historical importance comes from the involvement of police witnesses, the scale of civilian documentation and the unusually transparent military response. Those features make it stronger than a lone anecdote and more informative than folklore alone.

The case nevertheless falls short of proving that a single large triangular craft crossed the country. The strongest military episode lacked visual confirmation from the intercepting pilots and did not produce a stable, continuous radar track. The best-known photograph was fabricated. Other incidents were linked to lighting displays or remain susceptible to aircraft, astronomical, atmospheric and perceptual explanations.

The fairest conclusion is that the Belgian wave was a collection of different events interpreted through a rapidly consolidating shared image.
