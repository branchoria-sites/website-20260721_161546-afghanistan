## Introduction

[Sri Lanka]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-sri-lankas-ufo/' | relative_url }}) has never established a permanent, publicly accessible national programme for investigating unidentified flying objects (UFOs). Instead, reports have been examined intermittently by individual scientists, private research groups, journalists and, in some cases, through alleged military interest. The result is a fragmented documentary record: a handful of investigators have attempted to collect witness testimony and preserve case files, but there is no central archive comparable to the declassified collections released by countries such as the United Kingdom or the United States.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sundaytimes.lk/980510/plusm.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sundaytimes.lk">[The Sunday Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sundaytimes.lk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Sunday TimesThe Sunday Times Plus SectionMay 10, 1998...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 10, 1998</span></span></span>

This absence of official records shapes almost every discussion of Sri Lankan UFO cases. Many incidents remain unresolved not because they present compelling physical evidence, but because original witness statements, photographs, radar data, aviation records and government files are unavailable or were never systematically collected. Understanding who investigated these reports—and what records actually survive—is therefore more important than simply cataloguing sightings.

## Who investigated Sri Lanka's UFO reports?

### Chandana Jayaratne and scientific commentary

The most prominent scientific figure associated with Sri Lankan UFO investigations has been physicist Dr Chandana Jayaratne of the University of Colombo. During the publicity surrounding the [Bandarawela]({{ 'bandarawela/' | relative_url }}) sightings in 1998, he argued that unusual reports deserved careful investigation while also warning that most UFO claims ultimately prove to be misidentifications, atmospheric phenomena, psychological effects or deliberate hoaxes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sundaytimes.lk/980510/plusm.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sundaytimes.lk">[The Sunday Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sundaytimes.lk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Sunday TimesThe Sunday Times Plus SectionMay 10, 1998...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 10, 1998</span></span></span>

Rather than treating every report as evidence of extraterrestrial activity, Jayaratne promoted a structured investigative approach. Contemporary interviews describe plans to establish a dedicated reporting unit in cooperation with amateur astronomers and to create a public hotline so that sightings could be documented consistently. He also proposed minimum reporting standards, noting that isolated anecdotal claims were insufficient and that multiple independent witnesses greatly improved the value of a case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sundaytimes.lk/980510/plusm.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sundaytimes.lk">[The Sunday Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sundaytimes.lk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Sunday TimesThe Sunday Times Plus SectionMay 10, 1998...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 10, 1998</span></span></span>

His public position illustrates an important distinction within Sri Lankan UFO history. He neither dismissed every report outright nor accepted extraordinary explanations without evidence. Instead, he consistently argued that unexplained observations should be investigated scientifically while recognising that the overwhelming majority of cases have conventional explanations.

### The Sri Lanka UFO Research Association's role

Alongside academic commentary, private investigators attempted to create a more systematic record of sightings. The Sri Lanka UFO Research Association (often abbreviated as SLUFORA or referred to in contemporary newspapers as the UFO Society of Sri Lanka) became the country's principal civilian organisation devoted to collecting witness reports during the late 1990s and early 2000s.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sundaytimes.lk/000402/plus5.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sundaytimes.lk">[The Sunday Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sundaytimes.lk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sundaytimes.lk.</span></span></span>

The association encouraged members of the public to submit reports including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* date and time;
* duration of the sighting;
* weather conditions;
* observer location;
* viewing direction;
* nearby infrastructure such as power lines or airfields;
* whether binoculars or cameras were used.

</div>

These reporting guidelines broadly mirrored international civilian UFO investigation practices, emphasising documentation before interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sundaytimes.lk/000402/plus5.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sundaytimes.lk">[The Sunday Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sundaytimes.lk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on sundaytimes.lk.</span></span></span>

Much of what is now known about older Sri Lankan cases—including reported incidents at [Polonnaruwa]({{ 'polonnaruwa/' | relative_url }}), Bandarawela and elsewhere—derives from files attributed to this association rather than from government archives. However, many original case documents have never been widely published, making it difficult for independent researchers to verify witness interviews, investigative methods or later revisions.

## Why is there no accessible national UFO archive?

Unlike countries that operated formal defence or intelligence programmes and later declassified thousands of pages of records, Sri Lanka has never released an equivalent national UFO archive. Publicly available evidence does not indicate the existence of a long-running government office responsible for cataloguing unidentified aerial reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

Several factors contribute to this documentary gap:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **No permanent reporting system.** Sighting reports were usually handled by newspapers, private investigators or individual scientists rather than a standing state agency.
* **Limited preservation.** Witness accounts were often collected after media publicity rather than immediately after the event, increasing the risk of memory contamination.
* **Few technical records.** Publicly accessible radar logs, air traffic records, military flight data and meteorological analyses rarely accompany reported cases.
* **Restricted government transparency.** Even if individual reports reached authorities, there is little evidence that any investigative files entered a public archive.

</div>

The result is that many frequently cited Sri Lankan incidents survive only as newspaper accounts, secondary summaries or later retellings instead of complete investigative files.

## What is known about military and state inquiries?

Claims occasionally appear that the Sri Lanka Air Force or other government bodies examined unusual aerial sightings. However, the publicly available evidence is considerably thinner than these claims often suggest.

There is no released collection of Air Force investigative files comparable to Britain's former Ministry of Defence UFO archive or the United States Air Force's Project Blue Book records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National Archives...</span></span></span>

Some sightings were reportedly brought to the attention of military personnel, particularly during periods of heightened public interest such as the Bandarawela episode or later waves of unusual light reports. However, no publicly accessible collection of official reports, technical assessments, radar analyses or formal conclusions has emerged from these episodes.

Consequently, references to military involvement generally rely on newspaper reporting, witness recollections or statements from civilian investigators rather than released government documentation.

## How missing records affect the credibility of famous cases

The lack of original documentation creates recurring problems when assessing Sri Lankan UFO history.

A notable example is the frequently discussed Polonnaruwa case. Different sources disagree over the year of the incident, variously placing it in the 1980s or 1994. Some accounts describe a blue spherical object and a reported humanoid encounter, while others present different details. Without access to the original witness interviews or investigative file, it is impossible to determine which version—if any—is most accurate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sundaytimes.lk/980510/plusm.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sundaytimes.lk">[The Sunday Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sundaytimes.lk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Sunday TimesThe Sunday Times Plus SectionMay 10, 1998...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 10, 1998</span></span></span>

The Bandarawela wave presents a similar challenge. It generated multiple eyewitness accounts across several locations, attracting significant public attention and scientific interest. Yet no publicly available archive contains the complete set of witness statements, physical evidence, laboratory analyses or official technical evaluations that would allow later researchers to independently reassess the case.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sundaytimes.lk/980510/plusm.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sundaytimes.lk">[The Sunday Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sundaytimes.lk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Sunday TimesThe Sunday Times Plus SectionMay 10, 1998...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 10, 1998</span></span></span>

These documentary gaps do not prove that sightings were fabricated. Equally, they prevent the cases from serving as strong evidence for extraordinary explanations.

## What can researchers rely on today?

Researchers examining Sri Lankan UFO history must piece together evidence from several imperfect sources rather than a single authoritative archive.

The most useful surviving material includes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* contemporary newspaper reporting, particularly from periods of heightened public interest;
* interviews with investigators such as Chandana Jayaratne;
* case summaries produced by civilian UFO organisations;
* later historical reviews comparing multiple reports.

</div>

Each source has strengths and weaknesses. Newspapers preserve contemporary chronology but may prioritise dramatic accounts. Private investigators often gathered more detailed witness testimony but did not always publish complete documentation. Retrospective summaries help reconstruct broader patterns but sometimes introduce inconsistencies in dates or descriptions.

For this reason, the strongest historical approach is comparative rather than relying on any single narrative.

## The central lesson from Sri Lanka's investigation record

Sri Lanka's UFO history is notable less for spectacular official discoveries than for the absence of enduring official documentation. A small number of scientists and civilian investigators attempted to create systematic records, and organisations such as the Sri Lanka UFO Research Association provided an organised framework for collecting witness testimony. Yet these efforts never developed into a permanent national archive, and most underlying investigative material remains inaccessible or has not survived in publicly verifiable form.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sundaytimes.lk/980510/plusm.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sundaytimes.lk">[The Sunday Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sundaytimes.lk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Sunday TimesThe Sunday Times Plus SectionMay 10, 1998...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 10, 1998</span></span></span>

As a result, the country's UFO literature occupies an unusual middle ground. There are genuine reports, identifiable investigators and periods of organised inquiry, but there is no comprehensive body of official records capable of confirming, disproving or thoroughly re-evaluating most of the best-known cases. The missing archive is therefore not simply an administrative curiosity—it is the principal reason why many Sri Lankan UFO incidents remain debated decades after they were first reported.
