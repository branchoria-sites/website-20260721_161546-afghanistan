## Introduction

The so-called **Mosul Orb** is the best-known unresolved UAP case associated with Iraq, but the available public evidence is far more limited than many online discussions suggest. The case centres on a brief military recording reportedly captured over Mosul on 16 April 2016 by a US intelligence, surveillance and reconnaissance aircraft. A still image became public in early 2023, followed later by a very short video clip. Although the footage has genuine military provenance, it does not provide enough information to determine the object's size, distance, speed or behaviour with confidence. That distinction is crucial: the Mosul Orb remains unidentified because of missing data, not because the released imagery demonstrates extraordinary technology.

The case has become a useful illustration of a broader problem in UAP research. Even professionally collected military imagery can remain inconclusive when essential contextual information—such as camera geometry, tracking data, wind conditions, altitude and sensor metadata—is unavailable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceUAP - NASA ScienceFebruary 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 23, 2026</span></span></span>

## How the footage became public

Public awareness of the Mosul Orb began in January 2023, when investigative journalists Jeremy Corbell and George Knapp released a still frame that they said originated from a classified US government briefing on unidentified anomalous phenomena. According to their reporting, the image came from a short full-motion video recorded during military operations over Mosul in April 2016.

The image attracted immediate attention because it appeared to show a metallic-looking sphere without obvious wings, rotors or propulsion. Unlike many internet UFO photographs, it was presented as imagery originating from an authenticated military intelligence system rather than an anonymous civilian submission.

The case gained additional prominence during public discussions surrounding the US government's investigation of unidentified anomalous phenomena. A short clip related to the incident later entered the public domain, confirming that the still image had been extracted from a brief moving sequence rather than representing a single isolated photograph. Even with the video available, however, the recording lasts only a few seconds and contains little additional information beyond confirming that the object passed through the field of view.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceUAP - NASA ScienceFebruary 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 23, 2026</span></span></span>

## What the released footage can—and cannot—establish

The public material establishes several points with reasonable confidence.

The recording appears to have been captured by a genuine military surveillance platform during operations over Mosul in 2016. The object appears approximately spherical in the available imagery and lacks obvious aerodynamic features visible at the released resolution.

Beyond those basic observations, the evidence becomes much weaker.

The public clip does **not** reveal:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the object's actual range from the aircraft;
* its physical diameter;
* its altitude above the ground;
* its true airspeed;
* wind conditions;
* whether operators maintained radar or optical tracking;
* whether additional classified sensors observed it simultaneously.

</div>

These missing variables matter because almost every quantity that would distinguish an unusual craft from an ordinary airborne object depends on accurate range measurements. A small nearby balloon and a much larger distant object can appear nearly identical in a single camera image.

Likewise, apparent motion across the frame cannot be converted into real speed without knowing the relative motion of both the surveillance aircraft and the target. A moving camera observing a stationary or slowly drifting object can create surprisingly misleading visual impressions.

This is why analysts from both sceptical and more open-minded perspectives generally agree on one point: the released footage alone cannot support precise claims about extraordinary manoeuvres or advanced performance.

## Why identification remains difficult

Military full-motion video is often assumed to provide definitive answers, but the Mosul Orb demonstrates the opposite. Intelligence imagery is optimised for operational surveillance, not necessarily for later scientific analysis.

Several factors complicate interpretation:

**Compressed public footage.** The publicly available clip appears to be a heavily compressed extract rather than the original sensor recording, reducing fine detail.

**Missing metadata.** Camera zoom, viewing angle, aircraft position and sensor calibration have not been released publicly.

**Single viewpoint.** Without stereoscopic views or independent observations, distance cannot be measured directly.

**Unknown tracking history.** It is unclear whether the object was continuously observed before or after the released segment.

These limitations prevent investigators from calculating trajectory, acceleration or aerodynamic performance with confidence.

NASA's independent UAP study reached a broader conclusion directly relevant to cases like Mosul: most unresolved incidents remain unresolved because high-quality, complete observational data are absent rather than because they necessarily indicate exotic technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceUAP - NASA ScienceFebruary 23, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 23, 2026</span></span></span>

## Balloon, drone and parallax explanations

Because the available evidence is incomplete, several conventional explanations remain plausible.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Iraq_1aed9e_mosul_orb_evidence_3511a6-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Mosul Orb illustration 2" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Iraq_1aed9e_mosul_orb_evidence_3511a6-Illustration-2-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Iraq_1aed9e_mosul_orb_evidence_3511a6-Illustration-2-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="lazy" decoding="async" fetchpriority="low">

### Balloon hypothesis

The balloon explanation is widely regarded as one of the strongest conventional possibilities.

Conflict zones such as northern Iraq routinely contain weather balloons, surveillance balloons, advertising balloons and improvised lighter-than-air objects. At long distances, a spherical balloon can appear metallic due to sunlight reflecting from its surface.

A drifting balloon can also appear to move rapidly across the camera's field if the observing aircraft is travelling at high speed. Without precise wind data and range measurements, the apparent motion seen in the clip cannot distinguish a drifting balloon from an independently powered object.

Importantly, nothing in the released footage rules this explanation out.

### Drone hypothesis

Another possibility is that the object was a small unmanned aerial vehicle.

By 2016, both coalition forces and armed groups operating in Iraq were using a variety of commercial and improvised drones. However, the public imagery does not reveal obvious structural features such as propellers or wings.

That absence is not decisive. At sufficient distance and resolution, many small drones lose visible detail and may appear as simple dark or bright shapes.

Conversely, the footage also contains no positive evidence demonstrating powered flight beyond ordinary drone capabilities.

### Parallax and camera geometry

Parallax is among the most important explanations considered by image analysts.

Parallax occurs when the observer moves relative to an object. A surveillance aircraft travelling rapidly while looking sideways can produce apparent motion that greatly exceeds the target's actual movement.

Without complete flight data, analysts cannot determine how much of the observed motion belongs to the object itself and how much results from the aircraft's own movement.

Many apparently unusual military videos have ultimately proved sensitive to these geometric effects once full tracking information became available. The Mosul Orb lacks the necessary data for such reconstruction.

## Does the footage show anything extraordinary?

The answer depends on what standard of evidence is applied.

If the question is whether the recording shows an unidentified object, the answer is yes. The object has not been publicly identified with certainty.

If the question is whether the available evidence demonstrates technology beyond known aircraft or natural phenomena, the answer is no.

No publicly released measurements establish:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* impossible acceleration;
* instantaneous changes in direction;
* hypersonic flight;
* absence of aerodynamic interaction;
* extraordinary endurance;
* exotic propulsion.

</div>

Those are precisely the kinds of observations that would be required before concluding that the object displayed genuinely anomalous performance.

Instead, the case remains unresolved because the evidence is incomplete.

## Why the Mosul Orb still matters

The Mosul Orb has become significant less because of what it proves than because of what it illustrates about modern UAP investigations.

Unlike many famous UFO photographs, the case involves imagery reportedly originating from a military intelligence system operating over an active combat zone. That provenance gives the case greater credibility than anonymous online submissions, but it does not compensate for missing technical information.

The incident also demonstrates the difference between **authenticated source material** and **confirmed interpretation**. Authentic footage can still be impossible to identify conclusively if essential contextual data remain classified or unavailable.

Within Iraq's relatively limited catalogue of documented UAP incidents, the Mosul Orb therefore occupies an unusual middle ground. It is stronger than most anecdotal reports because its military origin appears credible, yet it falls well short of demonstrating extraordinary technology. The most evidence-based assessment remains that the object is genuinely **unidentified**, while conventional explanations—including a balloon, a drone or an object whose apparent motion is influenced by parallax—remain viable and cannot presently be excluded.
