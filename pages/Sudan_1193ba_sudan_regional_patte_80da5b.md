## Introduction

Regional differences in reported UFO sightings across [Sudan]({{ 'what-really-explains-sudans-ufo/' | relative_url }}) are better explained by differences in observation, communication and verification than by evidence that particular parts of the country experience more unusual aerial phenomena. The country's geography combines densely populated urban centres, vast desert regions, active military zones, limited communications infrastructure and changing air traffic patterns. These factors strongly influence who can report an unusual object, whether photographs are captured, and whether independent investigators can later identify what was seen.

Sudan's best-known modern unidentified aerial event—the luminous object observed over Khartoum in January 2018—illustrates this pattern. The sighting was widely photographed because it occurred near the country's largest urban area and was later matched by satellite observers to activity associated with a Falcon 9 upper-stage mission. The case demonstrates how location, observer density and access to independent analysis can determine whether an event is eventually explained rather than remaining an enduring mystery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/explore-by-time-period/postwar/ufo-reports/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[The National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The National Archives UFO reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The National ArchivesUFO reports - The National Archives...</span></span></span>

## Do some regions produce more UFO reports?

Available public evidence does not show that any Sudanese region consistently produces more genuinely unexplained aerial phenomena. Instead, reporting conditions differ sharply across the country.

Several overlapping factors influence where reports emerge:

## Khartoum's reporting advantage

Khartoum has long held the strongest reporting advantage for entirely practical reasons rather than paranormal ones.

Before the civil war, the capital contained Sudan's largest concentration of residents, government institutions, universities, media organisations and internet users. Any unusual object visible over the city had a much greater chance of being photographed by multiple independent witnesses than a similar event over sparsely populated terrain.

The January 2018 aerial display demonstrates this advantage. Numerous observers captured images, regional media reported the event quickly, and independent satellite trackers were able to compare witness timing with known orbital activity. That combination eventually produced a credible aerospace explanation involving the Falcon 9 upper stage rather than leaving the observation permanently unidentified.

Khartoum's position beneath major aviation routes also increases the likelihood that residents observe aircraft, high-altitude contrails, satellites, rocket exhaust plumes and other legitimate aerospace activity that can initially appear unusual under certain lighting conditions.

The recent conflict complicates this picture. Military aircraft, drones, air-defence activity and strikes around Khartoum and its airport create additional sources of unfamiliar lights or sounds, while damaged communications networks and restricted access make careful investigation considerably more difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/domains/air-operations/czibs/czib-2023-01r6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: easa.europa.eu">[europa.eu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">easa.europa.eu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">EASAAirspace of Sudan &#124; EASA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">EASAAirspace of Sudan &#124; EASA</span></span></span>

## Identification challenges across different regions

### Western Sudan and Darfur

Darfur presents almost the opposite reporting environment from pre-war Khartoum.

Large distances between settlements reduce the number of independent observers who might witness the same event. Since 2023, conflict has also restricted movement by journalists, humanitarian organisations and researchers, making verification especially difficult.

Military aircraft, drones, flares and explosions may all be visible over long distances, particularly at night. In the absence of multiple independent observations or detailed imagery, distinguishing between military activity and unusual atmospheric or astronomical phenomena becomes challenging.

Communication outages have further reduced the likelihood that local observations become part of any accessible public record.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.euaa.europa.eu/coi/sudan/2025/security-situation/13-impact-security-situation-civilian-population/132-communication-and-media-presence" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: euaa.europa.eu">[europa.eu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">euaa.europa.eu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on europa.eu.</span></span></span>

### Northern desert regions

Northern Sudan offers exceptionally dark skies with very low levels of artificial light. Such conditions improve visibility of meteors, satellites, the International Space Station, rocket exhaust illuminated by the Sun below the horizon and other high-altitude objects.

However, the same remoteness that produces excellent observing conditions also limits documentation. A striking aerial event may have only one or two witnesses, making later investigation extremely difficult.

Without photographs, precise timing or multiple observation points, many otherwise identifiable events cannot be reconstructed confidently.

### Eastern Sudan and the Red Sea corridor

Eastern Sudan, including the Port Sudan region, lies closer to important maritime and aviation corridors than much of the country's interior.

Aircraft operating over the Red Sea, satellites following common orbital paths and occasional rocket-related phenomena may therefore be observed more frequently than in isolated inland regions. Yet there is no evidence that this translates into a higher rate of genuinely unexplained events.

Instead, the area illustrates an important principle in UFO reporting: regions with more visible aerospace activity naturally produce more opportunities for misidentification.

## Conflict changes what observers see

Since civil war began in 2023, regional reporting conditions have changed dramatically.

Military aircraft, drones and air-defence systems have become much more prominent in several regions. International aviation authorities now warn that Sudanese airspace presents serious risks because of ongoing military operations and anti-aircraft weaponry. These conditions increase the number of unfamiliar aerial objects that civilians may observe while simultaneously reducing opportunities for safe, independent investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.easa.europa.eu/en/domains/air-operations/czibs/czib-2023-01r6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: easa.europa.eu">[EASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">easa.europa.eu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">EASAAirspace of Sudan &#124; EASA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">EASAAirspace of Sudan &#124; EASA</span></span></span>

At the same time:

* telecommunications infrastructure has suffered extensive damage;<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.etcluster.org/country/sudan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: etcluster.org">[etcluster.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">etcluster.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

## Why apparent hotspots cannot yet be measured

Unlike countries with decades of centralised UFO reporting, Sudan has no comprehensive national database that records sightings using consistent criteria.

There is also no publicly accessible archive allowing researchers to compare reports by state, population density or observation conditions. Many historical reports survive only in newspaper articles, personal recollections or social media posts, while numerous observations likely went unrecorded entirely.

This creates several statistical problems:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* reports cannot be normalised by regional population;
* observer density differs enormously between regions;
* communication failures create major reporting gaps;
* conflict has interrupted ordinary civilian documentation;
* many cases lack sufficient information for later identification.

</div>

Consequently, any map claiming that one Sudanese region is a UFO "hotspot" should be treated with considerable scepticism unless supported by a transparent dataset and independent analysis.

## What regional patterns actually suggest

The strongest conclusion supported by current evidence is not that some parts of Sudan experience more unexplained aerial phenomena, but that some parts are far better equipped to report and investigate them.

Urban centres such as Khartoum historically produced more documented sightings because they combined larger populations, greater media presence and better communications. Remote deserts offered excellent viewing conditions but relatively few documented observations.
