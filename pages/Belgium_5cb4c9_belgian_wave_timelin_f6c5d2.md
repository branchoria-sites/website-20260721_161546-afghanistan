## Introduction

The Belgian UFO wave was not a single night of unusual sightings but a prolonged surge of reports that unfolded unevenly across the country between late 1989 and 1991. It began with a concentrated cluster around Eupen in eastern [Belgium]({{ 'what-really-happened-during-belgiums-ufo/' | relative_url }}) before spreading across much of Wallonia, reaching the Brussels area and parts of Brabant, while remaining noticeably less intense in much of Flanders. Understanding this geographical and chronological pattern is essential because the wave was driven by reports rather than by a single confirmed physical object. The changing distribution of sightings reflects a combination of genuine witness experiences, media attention, police reporting, civilian investigations and public awareness, making the Belgian wave one of the best-documented examples of how a national UFO reporting episode can evolve over time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cobeps.org/pdf/belgian_wave_130310.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cobeps.org">[cobeps.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cobeps.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">belgian wave 130310</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">versionMarch 11, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 11, 2013</span></span></span>

## The Eupen reports of November 1989

The modern Belgian wave is generally dated to the evening of **29 November 1989**, when police officers near Eupen, close to the German border, reported observing an unusually large illuminated object for an extended period. Their accounts were quickly joined by numerous reports from civilians and additional police patrols in the surrounding region.

What distinguished this opening phase was not simply the credibility often attributed to the witnesses but the concentration of reports within a relatively small geographical area. Civilian investigators from the Belgian Society for the Study of Space Phenomena (SOBEPS) later documented well over one hundred reports associated with that evening, although published totals differ depending on whether investigators counted individual witnesses, locations or separate observation episodes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/intl-be-sobeps-vague-ovni-volume-1-sobeps-vague-ovni-belge-volume-1" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">intl be sobeps vague ovni volume 1 sobeps vague ovni belge volume 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencySOBEPS Vague d&#x27;OVNI sur la Belgique, Volume 1 (1989–1991), SOBEPS (Société Belge d&#x27;Étude des Phénomènes Spatiaux) · 1991...</span></span></span>

## How the wave spread across Wallonia and Brussels

Following the Eupen cluster, reports no longer remained confined to eastern Belgium. During December 1989 and into 1990, sightings appeared across much of southern and central Belgium.

The strongest concentration remained in **Liège Province**, but reports increasingly appeared around:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Namur;
* Brabant;
* the Brussels region;
* La Louvière and neighbouring communities in Hainaut;
* other parts of central Wallonia.

</div>

Mapping published by COBEPS, the successor organisation preserving much of the Belgian catalogue, shows that observations were widely distributed across southern Belgium rather than centred on a single locality. Several dates produced particularly dense clusters, with 29 November 1989 standing out as the most dramatic initial surge. Later bursts occurred on individual evenings during December 1989 and again during 1990 and early 1991.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cobeps.org/fr/vague-belge" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cobeps.org">[cobeps.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cobeps.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">La vague belge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La vague belge...</span></span></span>

This geographical spread matters because it demonstrates that the Belgian wave was not simply a local incident repeated by neighbouring witnesses. Instead, reports accumulated over many months from communities separated by considerable distances, even though the highest reporting density remained in eastern and central Wallonia.

## Why Wallonia produced more reports than Flanders

One striking feature of the Belgian wave is its uneven regional distribution.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.cobeps.org/pdf/belgian_wave_130310.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cobeps.org">[cobeps.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cobeps.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">belgian wave 130310</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">versionMarch 11, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 11, 2013</span></span></span>

Although reports came from across Belgium, the overwhelming majority originated in the country's French-speaking south rather than in Dutch-speaking Flanders. Several factors probably contributed to this imbalance.

First, the earliest highly publicised police observations occurred in eastern Wallonia, creating immediate regional awareness. Second, SOBEPS—the principal civilian investigation group—had particularly strong organisational networks in French-speaking Belgium, making it easier for witnesses there to submit reports and for investigators to collect detailed case files. Third, media attention itself may have encouraged additional witnesses to recognise and report unusual lights that might otherwise have gone unreported.

None of these factors necessarily means that unusual aerial phenomena occurred only in Wallonia. Instead, they demonstrate an important principle in analysing UFO databases: report density is influenced not only by what people observe but also by where reporting systems, investigators and publicity are strongest.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cobeps.org/fr/vague-belge" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cobeps.org">[cobeps.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cobeps.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">La vague belge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La vague belge...</span></span></span>

## The changing rhythm of reports

The Belgian wave is often remembered as a dramatic event lasting only a few months, but the chronology was more gradual.

Rather than rising steadily, reports arrived in bursts separated by quieter periods. Investigators identified several evenings during which numerous independent reports were submitted, while many intervening days produced only isolated observations.

Published catalogues also show that reporting continued beyond the famous F-16 interception attempt of **30–31 March 1990**. Although public attention increasingly focused on that military episode, civilian reports continued into 1991 before declining significantly. COBEPS identifies March 1991 as another notable reporting period, demonstrating that the wave did not end immediately after the most famous military incident.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cobeps.org/fr/vague-belge" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cobeps.org">[cobeps.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cobeps.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">La vague belge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La vague belge...</span></span></span>

This longer timeline complicates simple explanations. A short-lived media panic might produce a brief spike in reports, whereas Belgium experienced recurring clusters over more than a year. At the same time, the persistence of reports does not itself establish that witnesses observed the same underlying phenomenon.

## What the report totals actually mean

Large numerical claims are among the most misunderstood aspects of the Belgian wave.<span class="citation-chip-wrap"><a class="citation-chip" href="https://www.cobeps.org/pdf/belgian_wave_130310.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cobeps.org">[cobeps.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cobeps.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">belgian wave 130310</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">versionMarch 11, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 11, 2013</span></span></span>

Different publications refer to:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* thousands of witnesses;
* around two thousand reported sightings;
* several hundred investigated cases;
* catalogues containing hundreds of individual reports.

</div>

These figures describe different things and should not be treated as interchangeable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cobeps.org/pdf/belgian_wave_130310.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cobeps.org">[cobeps.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cobeps.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">belgian wave 130310</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">versionMarch 11, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 11, 2013</span></span></span>

For example:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Witnesses** count people who said they observed something.
* **Reports** count notifications submitted to investigators or authorities.
* **Cases** usually combine multiple witnesses describing what investigators believe was the same event.
* **Catalogues** contain investigated entries rather than every telephone call or newspaper story.

</div>

Similarly, annual totals published by Belgian investigators—such as hundreds of catalogued reports during 1989 and 1990 followed by a sharp decline in later years—measure reporting activity rather than verified unidentified craft. ## What regional patterns can—and cannot—tell us

The geographical progression from Eupen into wider Wallonia and central Belgium has encouraged competing interpretations.

Supporters of an unexplained phenomenon argue that the consistency of many witness descriptions across separated locations suggests more than isolated misidentifications. They also point to police reports and the willingness of Belgian authorities to examine the cases seriously.

More cautious analysts note that regional reporting patterns are shaped by social processes as well as by whatever was originally observed. Once the Eupen incidents became national news, witnesses elsewhere had a common vocabulary—especially the image of a large silent triangle—for describing unusual lights. This does not prove that later reports were mistaken, but it makes it difficult to separate independent observations from the influence of publicity and expectation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cobeps.org/pdf/belgian_wave_130310.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cobeps.org">[cobeps.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cobeps.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">belgian wave 130310</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">versionMarch 11, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 11, 2013</span></span></span>

## Why the Belgian wave remains historically important

Viewed across regions and years, the Belgian UFO wave stands out less because it produced definitive proof of extraordinary craft than because it generated an unusually rich record of how a national reporting wave develops.

Beginning with a concentrated cluster near Eupen, spreading across much of Wallonia and into Brussels, then gradually declining after 1991, the chronology reveals both remarkable consistency in many witness descriptions and the powerful influence of reporting networks, media coverage and organised civilian investigation.

For historians and researchers, the regional evolution is therefore one of the wave's most valuable features. It allows comparisons between where reports originated, how they spread, when they peaked and how they eventually faded—showing that the history of the Belgian wave is fundamentally the history of changing patterns of observation and reporting rather than a simple count of unexplained objects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cobeps.org/fr/vague-belge" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cobeps.org">[cobeps.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cobeps.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">La vague belge</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">La vague belge...</span></span></span>
