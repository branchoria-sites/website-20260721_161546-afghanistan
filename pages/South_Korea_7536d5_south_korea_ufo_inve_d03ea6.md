## Introduction

[South Korea]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-south-koreas/' | relative_url }}) has no permanent civilian agency dedicated to investigating unidentified flying object (UFO) or unidentified anomalous phenomenon (UAP) reports. Unlike the former US Air Force Project Blue Book or more recent official UAP offices established elsewhere, South Korean reports are examined through a patchwork of military responses, journalists, documentary filmmakers, academics and private enthusiasts. As a result, the quality of documentation varies widely. Some incidents generate military records because they involve protected airspace or air-defence activity, while many others survive only through witness interviews or television reconstructions. This fragmented system makes it difficult to compare cases consistently or to distinguish between well-supported reports and stories that have grown through repetition rather than new evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

## Journalists increasingly treat the subject as investigative reporting

South Korean journalism has gradually shifted from treating UFO stories purely as entertainment towards applying investigative methods to selected cases.

A notable example is journalist **Kim Young-nam**, whose book *UFO Is a Physical Object!* compiles interviews with researchers, military witnesses and international specialists while attempting to distinguish documented evidence from speculation. Rather than functioning as an official investigation, this work reflects an emerging journalistic approach that emphasises source evaluation, documentary records and comparison with overseas UAP developments.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://dl.nanet.go.kr/detail/MONO12022000073717" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dl.nanet.go.kr">[National Assembly Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dl.nanet.go.kr</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on go.kr.</span></span></span>

Responsible reporting in this area typically attempts to answer several practical questions before suggesting unusual explanations:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Was the object identified by aviation authorities?
* Were military exercises occurring nearby?
* Do astronomical or meteorological explanations fit?
* Are multiple independent witnesses available?
* Is there original photographic or radar evidence with a documented chain of custody?

</div>

Because these questions are not answered through a single national review process, different investigators sometimes reach different conclusions from the same incident.

## Television documentaries preserve testimony but rarely settle cases

Television has played an important role in preserving accounts that might otherwise disappear. Documentary programmes have recorded interviews with civilian witnesses, military veterans and local residents describing incidents that were never formally archived.

These productions provide valuable historical material because many eyewitnesses are interviewed decades after the events. However, they also illustrate the limitations of retrospective investigation. Memories may have changed, supporting documents may have been lost, and radar records or operational logs are often unavailable.

Modern documentary films such as *UFO Sketch* explicitly acknowledge this tension. Rather than claiming definitive proof, they show investigators attempting to verify stories through repeat visits, interviews and comparisons with available records, while frequently concluding that the available evidence is insufficient for firm conclusions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.koreanfilm.or.kr/eng/films/index/filmsView.jsp?movieCd=20191785" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: koreanfilm.or.kr">[Korean Film Council]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">koreanfilm.or.kr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">films View.jsp</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Korean Film CouncilUFO Sketch (2021)March 4, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 4, 2021</span></span></span>

For historians, these documentaries are therefore best viewed as collections of testimony rather than replacements for contemporaneous official documentation.

## What role does the government play?

South Korea does not maintain a publicly known office that routinely accepts and publishes civilian UFO reports.

Government agencies instead become involved when an aerial event overlaps with established responsibilities such as:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Air defence.
* National security.
* Aviation safety.
* Possible incursions by drones or foreign aircraft.
* Missile launches or unidentified radar contacts.

</div>

In such situations, investigations are conducted to determine whether the object represents a security threat rather than to evaluate extraterrestrial hypotheses. Public statements generally focus on identifying aircraft, balloons, drones or other conventional explanations whenever evidence permits. Recent Ministry of National Defense briefings concerning unidentified aerial activity demonstrate this security-oriented approach, coordinating with other government agencies where necessary instead of operating a dedicated UFO programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.korea.kr/news/policyNewsView.do?newsId=156739183" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: korea.kr">[Korea.kr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">korea.kr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">무인기 관련 북 총참모부 성명에 대한 국방부 입장발표</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">정책뉴스 &#124; 뉴스 &#124; 대한민국 정책브리핑January 10, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 10, 2026</span></span></span>

This differs significantly from countries that periodically release dedicated UAP reports or maintain public reporting mechanisms.

## Why South Korea lacks a central UFO archive

One of the defining characteristics of South Korean UFO research is the absence of a comprehensive, publicly searchable national archive.

Instead, information is scattered across several sources:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--phrase-pills" markdown="1">

* Newspaper archives.
* Television broadcasts.
* Private investigator collections.
* Academic commentary.
* Foreign military archives.
* Online witness submissions.

</div>

This fragmentation creates several practical problems.

First, different investigators may unknowingly analyse the same incident from incomplete records. Second, original photographs or videos are sometimes lost while copies continue circulating online without reliable provenance. Third, cases often become harder to verify as they are repeatedly retold through books, documentaries and internet discussions.

Unlike scientific databases that maintain standardised metadata and preserve original source material, South Korea's UFO documentation has largely evolved through independent collection efforts rather than coordinated institutional archiving.

## Evidence standards remain the central challenge

The greatest limitation facing investigators is not a shortage of reports but inconsistent evidence.

Experienced researchers increasingly distinguish between several categories:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Documented incidents**, supported by official records, multiple witnesses or contemporaneous reporting.
* **Contested cases**, where testimony exists but important corroborating evidence is missing.
* **Explained events**, later identified as aircraft, astronomical phenomena, rockets, drones or atmospheric effects.
* **Unresolved reports**, where available information is simply too limited for a confident conclusion.

</div>

An unresolved case is not automatically evidence of extraordinary technology. It often reflects missing data, poor image quality, incomplete records or the inability to reconstruct the circumstances years later. This cautious distinction mirrors the conclusions reached by Project Blue Book, whose archived Korean War cases remain important historical resources despite the programme's overall finding that its unidentified reports did not constitute evidence of extraterrestrial vehicles.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

## The key takeaway

The investigation of UFO claims in South Korea is decentralised. Independent researchers, investigative journalists and documentary filmmakers have preserved much of the country's UFO history, while government agencies become involved only when unidentified aerial events intersect with defence or aviation responsibilities. Because no public national archive applies consistent evidence standards across all reports, assessing individual cases requires careful comparison of eyewitness testimony with military records, aviation data, contemporary reporting and later investigative work. The result is a body of evidence that is historically valuable but uneven, making methodological caution as important as the sightings themselves.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.koreanfilm.or.kr/eng/films/index/filmsView.jsp?movieCd=20191785" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: koreanfilm.or.kr">[or.kr]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">koreanfilm.or.kr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">films View.jsp</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Korean Film CouncilUFO Sketch (2021)March 4, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 4, 2021</span></span></span>
