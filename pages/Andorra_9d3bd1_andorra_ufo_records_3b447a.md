## Introduction

[Andorra]({{ 'what-really-flew-over-andorra/' | relative_url }}) has no known public equivalent of a national UFO or Unidentified Aerial Phenomena (UAP) archive. Unlike neighbouring France, which maintains a long-running official reporting and investigation system through GEIPAN, Andorra has no publicly identified government body that routinely accepts civilian reports, investigates unusual aerial observations, classifies cases or publishes case files. As a result, nearly every reported UFO incident associated with the country relies on media reports, personal testimony or international civilian databases rather than official documentation.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Andorra_9d3bd1_andorra_ufo_records_3b447a-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" alt="Official Records illustration 1" data-theme-src-dark="{{ "/assets/images/Andorra_9d3bd1_andorra_ufo_records_3b447a-Illustration-1-dark.svg" | relative_url }}" data-theme-src-light="{{ "/assets/images/Andorra_9d3bd1_andorra_ufo_records_3b447a-Illustration-1-light.svg" | relative_url }}" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

This absence does not imply that unusual aerial observations never occur in Andorra. Instead, it means that there is no transparent institutional record against which claims can be checked. For anyone assessing Andorran UFO reports, the missing archive is itself one of the most important facts because it limits independent verification and makes the quality of individual sources far more significant.

## Why Andorra has never developed a public archive

Several structural factors help explain why no comparable archive has emerged.

First, Andorra is a microstate with a population of roughly 80,000 people and very limited administrative capacity compared with neighbouring countries. Governments of this size typically concentrate specialist resources on core public services rather than maintaining dedicated investigative units for extremely infrequent reports of unusual aerial phenomena.

Second, Andorra has no independent military or air force. National defence responsibilities are unusual by international standards, and the country does not operate the large defence bureaucracy that historically generated many UFO files elsewhere. In countries such as the United Kingdom or the United States, military reporting systems produced archives because armed forces routinely received and processed public reports. Those institutional pathways have never existed in the same way in Andorra.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/explore-by-time-period/postwar/ufo-reports/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[The National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The National Archives UFO reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The National ArchivesUFO reports - The National Archives...</span></span></span>

Third, the country's airspace is relatively small and surrounded by Spain and France. Aviation monitoring, meteorological information and broader air traffic awareness therefore occur within a wider regional context rather than through a large national aerospace infrastructure dedicated to Andorra alone.

Finally, there is little evidence of sustained political or public demand for a permanent UFO reporting office. The documented number of notable Andorran cases remains very small, reducing the practical incentive to establish a specialised archive.

## How France's investigation model differs

The strongest comparison is France, whose approach demonstrates what an official archive actually looks like.

France's GEIPAN (Groupe d'Études et d'Informations sur les Phénomènes Aérospatiaux Non identifiés) operates within the French national space agency CNES. Since its establishment in 1977, it has accepted witness reports, collected supporting evidence, carried out multidisciplinary investigations and published many case files online while protecting witness identities. Reports are evaluated using recognised scientific and investigative methods rather than assuming extraordinary explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/en/node/440" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

Key features include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* a standard reporting procedure for witnesses;
* structured questionnaires designed to collect comparable information;
* examination of astronomical, meteorological and aviation data;
* consultation with technical specialists where appropriate;
* publication of investigation results; and
* classification of cases according to the available evidence.

</div>

Importantly, GEIPAN is explicit that it does not investigate extraterrestrial life. Its purpose is to determine whether an aerial observation can be explained using available evidence and recognised scientific knowledge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.geipan.fr/en/node/58792" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: geipan.fr">[Geipan]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">geipan.fr</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Mission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Mission &amp; Geipan &#124; GEIPAN...</span></span></span>

No publicly documented Andorran institution performs these functions.

## What the missing archive means for Andorran sightings

The absence of official case files affects how every Andorran UFO claim should be interpreted.

Without an official investigation, researchers generally cannot verify:

* the exact time and location of an event;

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* whether multiple independent witnesses were interviewed;
* weather conditions;
* astronomical conditions;
* aircraft activity;
* satellite passes;
* radar information;
* photographic metadata; or
* whether alternative explanations were systematically excluded.

</div>

This helps explain why several of Andorra's better-known reports remain difficult to assess.

For example, retrospective [witness accounts]({{ 'witness-accounts/' | relative_url }}) published many years after the alleged observations lack contemporaneous official documentation. Likewise, incidents later shown to involve ordinary objects—such as the 1967 meteorological balloons—or deliberate hoaxes, such as the widely reported rectangular light seen from parts of southern France and Andorra in 2021, illustrate how easily unusual observations can enter public discussion before reliable documentation exists.

Without a national archive, these corrective investigations are often preserved only in newspaper reporting rather than in permanent government records.

## Why international UFO databases are not substitutes

Some readers assume that international UFO databases fill the gap left by the absence of an official archive. They do not.

Voluntary reporting databases provide useful catalogues of claimed observations, but they differ fundamentally from official investigative archives because:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* submissions are generally made directly by witnesses;
* reports may receive little or no independent verification;
* supporting evidence varies greatly in quality;
* duplicate reports can occur; and
* investigative standards are not uniform across countries.

</div>

Such databases can help identify patterns or preserve witness testimony, but they cannot replace a government investigation supported by aviation records, technical experts or official documentation.

For Andorra, this distinction is particularly important because these voluntary collections often become the primary accessible record of alleged sightings.

## What a credible Andorran case archive would require

If Andorra were ever to establish a public system for recording unusual aerial observations, credibility would depend less on the number of reported cases than on the quality and transparency of the process.

A robust archive would normally include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a standard witness reporting form;
* precise dates, times and locations;
* preservation of original photographs or videos and their metadata;
* checks against aircraft movements, satellites, astronomical objects and weather conditions;
* documentation of investigative methods;
* clear classifications distinguishing identified, probably identified, insufficient-information and genuinely unexplained cases;
* protection of witness privacy; and
* publication of completed investigations wherever legally possible.

</div>

The value of such an archive would not lie in proving extraordinary claims. Instead, it would provide a transparent evidential record that allows researchers, journalists and the public to distinguish between verified explanations, unresolved observations and unsupported stories.

## The central takeaway

The defining feature of Andorra's UFO record is not an abundance of unexplained incidents but the absence of an official documentary framework. Without a public archive, almost every reported case depends on secondary sources, retrospective testimony or voluntary reporting databases rather than government investigations.

For readers examining Andorran UFO claims, this means the first question is often not whether an observation was extraordinary, but whether any contemporaneous official documentation exists at all. In most known cases, the answer is no, making careful source evaluation more important than the claim itself.
