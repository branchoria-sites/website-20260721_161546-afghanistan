## Introduction

The absence of publicly available Liberian UFO files is best explained by incomplete institutional records rather than by evidence of either systematic government investigations or deliberate concealment. Unlike countries that established dedicated military or civilian programmes to log unidentified aerial phenomena (UAP), [Liberia]({{ 'what-do-we-really-know-about-liberias/' | relative_url }}) has no known public UAP office, no released catalogue of historical investigations, and no declassified collection comparable to those available in the United Kingdom or the United States. At the same time, Liberia's prolonged civil conflicts, damage to public institutions, and limited archival capacity provide a credible explanation for why historical aviation or security records may be fragmented or unavailable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/african-research-and-documentation/article/abs/visit-to-the-national-archives-of-liberia/95BCDA927E84B469AF50FCF2C9BEED47" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[cambridge.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentA Visit to the National Archives of Liberia &#124; African Research and Documentation &#124; Cambridge CoreA...</span></span></span>

This distinction is important. Missing records are not evidence that extraordinary incidents occurred, but neither do they prove that no unusual aerial observations were ever reported. The available evidence supports a more cautious conclusion: Liberia's archival history makes the documentary record incomplete, leaving researchers unable to determine whether routine reports were never created, later destroyed, or simply remain inaccessible.

## How civil conflict disrupted record survival

Liberia experienced two devastating civil wars between 1989 and 2003. Beyond their humanitarian consequences, these conflicts severely damaged government administration and the country's documentary heritage.

One of the clearest descriptions comes from historian Stephen Ellis, who documented the condition of the National Archives of Liberia. According to his account:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* the archives' original building was vandalised during the early stages of the first civil war;
* roughly 40% of archival holdings were reportedly destroyed;
* surviving records were repeatedly moved between temporary locations;
* some archives were stored under unsuitable conditions, including buildings used by peacekeeping forces;
* long-term preservation and cataloguing were substantially disrupted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/african-research-and-documentation/article/abs/visit-to-the-national-archives-of-liberia/95BCDA927E84B469AF50FCF2C9BEED47" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentA Visit to the National Archives of Liberia &#124; African Research and Documentation &#124; Cambridge CoreA...</span></span></span>

</div>

These documented losses matter when evaluating claims about missing UFO records. If routine administrative files from numerous ministries were damaged, dispersed or never fully reorganised after the wars, the absence of aviation or security reports today cannot automatically be interpreted as evidence of intentional suppression.

Instead, it reflects a broader archival problem affecting many areas of Liberian history.

## Why missing files are not evidence of a cover-up

A common misconception in UFO discussions is that an absence of official documentation necessarily indicates that records were deliberately hidden.

Liberia provides a useful example of why that reasoning is weak.

Several more ordinary explanations are consistent with the available evidence:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* no unusual event was ever formally reported;
* reports were handled as ordinary aviation or police matters rather than as UFO cases;
* records were created but later destroyed during conflict;
* files survived but remain uncatalogued or inaccessible;
* records were never transferred into a modern archival system.

</div>

None of these possibilities can presently be confirmed or excluded.

The documented deterioration of Liberia's national archives makes archival loss a plausible explanation, but it does not establish that specific UFO files once existed. Evidence of missing capacity should not be confused with evidence of missing incidents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/african-research-and-documentation/article/abs/visit-to-the-national-archives-of-liberia/95BCDA927E84B469AF50FCF2C9BEED47" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentA Visit to the National Archives of Liberia &#124; African Research and Documentation &#124; Cambridge CoreA...</span></span></span>

## What declassified records do—and do not—show

Countries with recognised official UFO programmes leave identifiable documentary trails.

For example:

* the UK Ministry of Defence preserved many UFO policy files, public correspondence and sighting reports before transferring surviving material to the National Archives;
* the United States declassified Project Blue Book records and later established additional processes for organising federal UAP records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/explore-by-time-period/postwar/ufo-reports/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[nationalarchives.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The National Archives UFO reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The National ArchivesUFO reports - The National Archives...</span></span></span>

No equivalent body of Liberian records has been identified in publicly accessible national archives, international declassification projects or recognised historical repositories.

This absence should not be interpreted as evidence that Liberia secretly operated a hidden UFO investigation programme. Rather, it indicates that researchers have not located documentary evidence showing that such a programme existed.

Equally, references to extensive American or British declassified material cannot be used as indirect evidence for Liberia. Each country's archival history must be assessed independently.

## The role of post-war documentation

One important distinction is that Liberia has invested significant effort in documenting the civil wars themselves.

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC), established after the conflict, gathered testimony and created an archive relating to wartime human rights abuses. The legislation establishing the Commission also specified that its archives would remain in the public domain except for confidential records protected for a defined period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.trcofliberia.org/about/trc-mandate.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: trcofliberia.org">[trcofliberia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">trcofliberia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Truth and Reconciliation Commission of Liberia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">May 12, 2005...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 12, 2005</span></span></span>

This demonstrates two relevant points:

First, Liberia has undertaken substantial archival work in areas considered nationally important.

Second, those efforts focused on documenting conflict, accountability and reconciliation rather than unexplained aerial phenomena.

The existence of well-defined TRC archives therefore does not imply the existence of comparable aviation or UAP collections.

## What researchers can reasonably conclude

The present evidence supports several careful conclusions.

There is **no publicly documented Liberian equivalent** of Project Blue Book, the UK Ministry of Defence UFO desk or another formal national UAP investigation programme.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/explore-by-time-period/postwar/ufo-reports/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[The National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The National Archives UFO reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The National ArchivesUFO reports - The National Archives...</span></span></span>

Liberia's documented archival losses during the civil wars make incomplete preservation of government records entirely plausible. That institutional history provides a credible explanation for why historical aviation or security records may be difficult—or impossible—to locate today.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/african-research-and-documentation/article/abs/visit-to-the-national-archives-of-liberia/95BCDA927E84B469AF50FCF2C9BEED47" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambridge.org">[Cambridge University Press &amp; Assessment]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambridge.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridge University Press &amp; AssessmentA Visit to the National Archives of Liberia &#124; African Research and Documentation &#124; Cambridge CoreA...</span></span></span>

However, the archival gap should not be overstated. No evidence currently demonstrates that Liberia once possessed extensive UFO files that were later destroyed, nor is there evidence that authorities systematically concealed such records.

For readers examining Liberia's wider UFO history, the archive gap is therefore best understood as a limitation on what historians can verify. It explains why the documentary record is unusually thin, but it does not itself support claims either for extraordinary aerial phenomena or for a government cover-up.
