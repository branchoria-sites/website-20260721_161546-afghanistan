## Introduction

The most frequently proposed conventional explanations for the 1959 Boianai sightings in [Papua New Guinea]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-papua-new/' | relative_url }}) are that the witnesses misidentified the planet Venus, ordinary aircraft, illuminated clouds or other familiar visual phenomena. These hypotheses deserve careful examination because they test whether an apparently extraordinary event can be explained without invoking an unknown aerial phenomenon. However, while some individual aspects of the reports may be compatible with ordinary causes, no single conventional explanation has gained broad acceptance as a complete account of what Reverend William Gill and the other witnesses described. The debate therefore centres less on whether natural or human-made phenomena were present, and more on whether they adequately explain the combination of reported lights, apparent manoeuvres and observations of human-like figures.

## The case for Venus and atmospheric distortion

The best-known sceptical explanation was proposed by the astronomer Donald H. Menzel, a prominent critic of UFO claims. Menzel argued that the principal light could have been the planet Venus, which was bright in the western evening sky during the relevant period. He further suggested that optical imperfections such as myopia, astigmatism and retinal effects might have caused the planet to appear larger, distorted or apparently mobile. Atmospheric turbulence could then have reinforced the illusion of movement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/htm/papua59.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[ufologie.patrickgross.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ufo - UFOs at close sight: Father Gill sighting, Papua - New Guinea, 1959...</span></span></span>

At first glance, this explanation has several strengths:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Venus is one of the brightest objects visible after sunset and is commonly mistaken for an unusual aerial object.
* Atmospheric refraction near the horizon can alter brightness, colour and apparent shape.
* The autokinetic effect—a well-documented psychological phenomenon in which a fixed light against a dark background appears to drift—can create convincing impressions of motion when observers lack stable visual references.

</div>

These mechanisms have explained numerous historical UFO reports elsewhere and therefore cannot be dismissed simply because the [Boianai case]({{ 'boianai-case/' | relative_url }}) is unusual.

However, the Venus hypothesis encounters significant difficulties when compared with Gill's original descriptions.

Gill's reports indicate that the witnesses watched the object over an extended period while also identifying other celestial objects. Later investigators noted that Gill reportedly distinguished Venus separately during at least part of the observations, reducing the likelihood that the principal object itself was Venus. Critics of Menzel's analysis have also disputed his assumption that Gill observed without adequate vision correction, noting that he was reportedly wearing his prescribed glasses.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/htm/papua59.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[ufologie.patrickgross.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ufo - UFOs at close sight: Father Gill sighting, Papua - New Guinea, 1959...</span></span></span>

More importantly, the central claim involved apparent figures standing on or near the object and what witnesses interpreted as deliberate signalling in response to people waving from the ground. While visual distortion can alter the appearance of a bright point source, it is considerably harder to explain repeated perceptions of structured forms and apparently coordinated actions solely through atmospheric optics.

## Aircraft, cloud and autokinetic effects

Aircraft have also been proposed as a possible explanation. Papua and New Guinea was under Australian administration in 1959, and civil and military aviation existed in the region. A distant aircraft viewed through haze or broken cloud can produce unusual lighting effects, particularly around dusk.

Yet several aspects make a straightforward aircraft explanation problematic:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The witnesses reported an apparently stationary luminous object for an extended period rather than a conventional flight path.
* No known aircraft type operating locally matched the reported behaviour.
* Contemporary investigations produced no confirmed aviation record corresponding to the observations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufologie.patrickgross.org/htm/papua59letters.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[ufologie.patrickgross.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ufo - UFOs at close sight: Father Gill sighting, Papua - New Guinea, 1959, the letters...</span></span></span>

</div>

Clouds nevertheless remain relevant to parts of the episode. Gill repeatedly described clouds obscuring and revealing the lights, and changing cloud thickness can create illusions of apparent movement, varying brightness and transient shapes. Reflected moonlight or starlight on cloud edges can also exaggerate perceived size or produce impressions of objects entering and leaving cloud banks.

The Royal Australian Air Force's assessment reflected this line of reasoning more broadly. Its investigators suggested that some lights could have been bright planets viewed through varying cloud conditions, with atmospheric refraction and cloud movement contributing to impressions of motion. The report also proposed that changing cloud density might have encouraged witnesses to interpret irregular shapes as human figures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufologie.patrickgross.org/htm/papua59letters.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[ufologie.patrickgross.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ufo - UFOs at close sight: Father Gill sighting, Papua - New Guinea, 1959, the letters...</span></span></span>

Psychological factors provide another partial explanation. Once one observer identifies a distant light as unusual, expectations can spread through a group. This does not require dishonesty; it reflects well-established processes of suggestion, selective attention and collective interpretation. Multi-witness cases are therefore not automatically immune from perceptual influences, especially when everyone observes the same distant stimulus under difficult viewing conditions.

## Why no conventional explanation fits every detail

The principal weakness shared by the major sceptical explanations is that each appears capable of accounting for only part of the reported observations.

The Venus hypothesis plausibly explains a bright stationary light but struggles with the reported signalling behaviour, apparent occupants and repeated descriptions extending across multiple evenings.

Aircraft explanations address the possibility of artificial lights yet fit poorly with the reported duration, apparent hovering and lack of corroborating aviation evidence.

Clouds and atmospheric distortion clearly influenced visibility during the observations and may explain changes in brightness or apparent shape, but they do not readily account for the more structured elements of the testimony.

Autokinetic and expectation effects offer psychologically plausible mechanisms for perceived movement, although they become less persuasive if numerous independent observers consistently reported the same complex sequence rather than merely a drifting point of light.

This mixed picture led even investigators sympathetic to conventional explanations to separate different components of the case. Later analysis associated with J. Allen Hynek's Centre for UFO Studies suggested that some of the smaller lights seen during the events could reasonably have been stars or planets, while concluding that the principal reported object was not satisfactorily explained by an astronomical identification alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufologie.patrickgross.org/htm/papua59report.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[ufologie.patrickgross.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ufo - UFOs at close sight: Father Gill sighting, Papua - New Guinea, 1959, detailed report...</span></span></span>

## A balanced assessment

Within the wider Papua New Guinea UFO record, the Boianai encounter illustrates the limits of both sceptical and extraordinary interpretations. Ordinary astronomical and atmospheric phenomena almost certainly formed part of the visual environment on the evenings in question, and they provide credible explanations for some reported features. Equally, the surviving witness testimony contains elements that are not easily reduced to Venus, aircraft or cloud effects without making additional assumptions about widespread perceptual error or memory distortion.

As a result, the strongest evidence-based conclusion is neither that the Boianai encounter demonstrates an extraordinary craft nor that it has been conclusively debunked. Instead, it remains a case in which conventional mechanisms plausibly explain portions of the observations, while leaving other reported details open to continued debate. That unresolved balance is one reason the Boianai incident continues to occupy a distinctive place within discussions of Papua New Guinea's UFO history.
