## Introduction

Drone reports in [Ghana]({{ 'what-really-lies-behind-ghanas-ufo/' | relative_url }})’s Volta Region show how modern aerial technology can create confusion between genuine security concerns and what some observers may interpret as unusual or unidentified aerial activity. The available evidence does not indicate that the region has a confirmed UFO event linked to these reports. Instead, the strongest documented cases involve unidentified drone operators near sensitive locations, prompting authorities to treat unknown aerial activity as a governance and security issue.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://gna.org.gh/2024/01/volta-regsec-re-cautions-public-on-unauthorised-drones-usage/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gna.org.gh">[Ghana National Association]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gna.org.gh</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Ghana National AssociationVolta REGSEC re-cautions public on unauthorised drones&#x27; usage &#124; Ghana News AgencyJanuary 10, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 10, 2024</span></span></span>

## Volta Region sightings and public warnings

The Volta Region drone reports became prominent after security authorities raised concerns about drones being flown near protected locations. These reports are important within Ghana’s wider UFO discussion because they demonstrate a recurring pattern: an unfamiliar object in the sky may remain “unidentified” to witnesses without necessarily being unexplained in a scientific sense. The missing information is usually practical — who operated it, why it was there, whether it had permission, and what equipment it carried.

## How drones create UFO-like confusion

### Small aircraft, limited visibility, uncertain purpose

Consumer drones are increasingly common because they are relatively affordable, easy to transport, and capable of carrying high-quality cameras. From a distance, many drones appear only as moving lights or small objects, especially at dusk or at night. Without information about the operator, observers may struggle to distinguish between a drone, a conventional aircraft, a star or planet, or another aerial object.

This creates a particular identification challenge in places where aerial activity near important sites is unusual. A resident seeing a hovering object near a government building, military facility, or prison may reasonably consider it suspicious, even if the object itself is a commercially available drone.

The Volta Region cases illustrate why identification depends on evidence rather than appearance alone. A serious investigation would require details such as the drone’s flight path, altitude, duration, operator identity, video recordings with metadata, and any available security observations. Without these details, the event remains a report of unauthorised aerial activity rather than proof of an unknown technology.

### Technology changes the meaning of “unidentified”

The term “UFO” simply describes something observed in the sky that has not yet been identified. It does not by itself establish an unusual origin. In the Volta Region, the documented uncertainty concerned ownership and authorisation rather than the physical nature of the objects.

This distinction matters because drones represent a relatively new category of aerial object. A person unfamiliar with drone flight characteristics may perceive hovering, sudden direction changes, or silent movement as unusual. However, these features are normal for many unmanned aircraft.

The Ghana Civil Aviation Authority (GCAA) recognises remotely piloted aircraft systems (RPAS), the formal term used for drones, as aircraft requiring regulation. Ghana’s RPAS framework includes requirements covering registration, operational approval, and restrictions on where drones may fly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gcaa.com.gh/web/remotely-piloted-aircraft-system-2/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gcaa.com.gh">[GCAA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gcaa.com.gh</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GCAAREMOTELY PILOTED AIRCRAFT SYSTEMS / DRONES</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GHANA CIVIL AVIATION AUTHORITYJune 25, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2019</span></span></span>

## Security and identification issues

The Volta Region reports highlight a practical security problem: authorities must respond to unknown aerial activity before they know whether it is harmless, accidental, commercial, or hostile.

### Why restricted areas create higher concern

Sensitive locations such as military facilities and prisons have different security requirements from ordinary public spaces. A drone flying near a private property may raise privacy questions, while a drone near a military installation can raise concerns about surveillance, intelligence gathering, or operational safety.

Ghana’s aviation regulations recognise this distinction. The GCAA has issued guidance on RPAS “no fly zones”, identifying certain locations as restricted because of security or public safety considerations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gcaa.com.gh/web/wp-content/uploads/2023/RPAS%20AOC/AC%2028-009%20-%20RPAS%20NO%20Fly%20Zones.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gcaa.com.gh">[GCAA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gcaa.com.gh</span><span class="citation-popover-title">AC 28 009 RPAS NO Fly Zones</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ADVISORY CIRCULARFebruary 15, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 15, 2023</span></span></span>

For security agencies, the challenge is therefore not simply identifying the object in the sky. It is determining:

* whether the operator has legal permission;

### The challenge of balancing security and legitimate drone use

Drones also have legitimate uses in Ghana, including photography, mapping, agriculture, surveying, emergency response, and security support. Completely restricting drone activity would limit beneficial applications. The policy challenge is creating a system where authorised users can operate while sensitive locations remain protected.

The GCAA’s RPAS guidance attempts to create this balance by requiring approval processes and operational controls rather than treating all drone flights as threats.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gcaa.com.gh/web/remotely-piloted-aircraft-system-2/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gcaa.com.gh">[GCAA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gcaa.com.gh</span><span class="citation-popover-title">GCAAREMOTELY PILOTED AIRCRAFT SYSTEMS / DRONES</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">GHANA CIVIL AVIATION AUTHORITYJune 25, 2019...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2019</span></span></span>
