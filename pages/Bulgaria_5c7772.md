## What Bulgaria’s UFO record actually contains

The term “UFO” means only that an object or light was unidentified by the observer at the time. It does not establish that the phenomenon was technologically extraordinary, still less extraterrestrial. In Bulgaria, the surviving public record contains three distinct categories: ordinary civilian sky reports; claims attributed to pilots or military personnel; and the Tsarichina excavation, which is often grouped with UFO history even though it concerned an underground search rather than a conventional aerial encounter.

No publicly accessible evidence currently shows that Bulgaria maintained a sustained, transparent national investigation comparable to a formal scientific observatory or a regularly published defence reporting programme. The country has extensive state and military archival institutions, including separate historical collections linked to the General Staff and central military archives, but publicly searchable UFO-specific catalogues are not evident. Consequently, much of the chronology has to be reconstructed from journalism, witness submissions and later recollections rather than complete operational files.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.government.bg/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.government.bg">[government.bg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.government.bg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Държавна агенция &quot;Архиви&quot;Държавна агенция &quot;Архиви&quot; (ДАА) е държавната институция в Република България, която осъществява в национален мащ...</span></span></span>

## A cautious chronology of reported incidents

Bulgaria does not have a dependable public master chronology extending continuously through the Cold War. The clearest evidence-supported timeline therefore begins with the episodes and reporting systems that can still be checked.

**1990–92: the Tsarichina military excavation.** Bulgarian military personnel excavated beneath the village of Tsarichina, north-west of Sofia, during the unsettled transition after communist rule. Accounts agree that the project began as a search connected to alleged treasure or a concealed historical object. Psychics then became influential, and the target changed in various retellings into a primordial human, a hermaphroditic extraterrestrial or an artefact capable of transforming knowledge about human origins. The tunnel was eventually abandoned and sealed without a publicly demonstrated discovery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsarichina" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

## Why the archive problem is so important

The usual claim that Bulgarian UFO evidence is “classified” is too simple. Bulgaria’s military archival history is institutionally complicated: operational, General Staff, central military and historical holdings have been reorganised over decades. Records may be transferred, catalogued under unexpected subjects, restricted for legitimate defence reasons or lost through weak administrative practice. The existence of multiple archives makes investigation possible, but it also means that the absence of a document from one repository cannot establish either deliberate destruction or extraterrestrial significance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://armymedia.bg/2018/06/22/%D1%81%D0%BF%D0%B8%D1%81%D1%8A%D0%BA-%D1%81-%D0%B8%D0%BC%D0%B5%D0%BD%D0%B0%D1%82%D0%B0-%D0%BD%D0%B0-%D0%B3%D0%B5%D1%80%D0%BE%D0%B8%D1%82%D0%B5-%D0%BE%D1%82-%D0%B2%D0%BE%D0%B9%D0%BD%D0%B8%D1%82%D0%B5/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: armymedia.bg">[armymedia.bg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">armymedia.bg</span><span class="citation-popover-title">списък с имената на героите от войните</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">списък с имената на героите от войните</span></span></span>

Foreign intelligence archives offer little help. CIA search results containing “Bulgaria” and “UFO” often prove to be broadcast-monitoring indexes, press lists or documents where the two terms occur in unrelated passages. Their appearance in an intelligence archive does not mean the CIA verified a Bulgarian sighting. Intelligence agencies routinely collected foreign media reporting, including material they did not endorse.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/docs/DOC_0005516692.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cia.gov">[CIA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cia.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">DOC 0005516692</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">DOC 0005516692</span></span></span>

None of the major Bulgarian claims currently has such a complete public package.

## How reliable are the main source types?

**State and military archives** are potentially the most valuable sources for air-defence incidents, but incomplete cataloguing and uncertain access limit what can presently be concluded. An official document would establish what authorities recorded or believed; it would not automatically establish that their interpretation was correct.

**Contemporaneous journalism** is useful for dates, named participants and the public evolution of a case. Bulgarian coverage of Tsarichina preserves important historical detail, but popular articles sometimes repeat increasingly elaborate paranormal claims without separating first-hand evidence from later legend.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vagabond.bg/bulgarian-x-files-4600" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vagabond.bg">[vagabond.bg]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vagabond.bg</span><span class="citation-popover-title">bulgarian x files 4600</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BULGARIAN X FILES28 Apr 2025 — By this time, the young, free Bulgarian media had heard about the Tsarichina Hole and were eager t...</span></span></span>

## The most reasonable assessment

Bulgaria has a genuine history of unidentified-object reporting, but its evidential base is fragmented and generally low-resolution. Civilian reports confirm that unusual lights and objects have been observed across the country. They do not form a consistent pattern of shape, behaviour or location that points to one extraordinary cause. The apparent emphasis on Sofia and the Black Sea coast is readily influenced by population, air traffic, tourism and visibility.

Tsarichina remains Bulgaria’s most unusual state-connected episode, yet its significance is institutional and cultural rather than extraterrestrial. It reveals how military authority, political transition, psychic belief, secrecy and poor documentation combined to create an enduring national mystery. The operation’s reality should not be confused with the truth of every claim attached to it.

The strongest Bulgarian lesson is the Haskovo case. An object can be genuinely seen, genuinely filmed and genuinely unidentified by witnesses, while still proving to be an ordinary satellite once timing and trajectory are checked. Bulgaria’s unresolved cases are therefore best regarded as incomplete investigations, not confirmed alien encounters. A future case would become substantially more persuasive only through independent multi-sensor records, precise metadata and transparent technical review rather than testimony or imagery alone.
