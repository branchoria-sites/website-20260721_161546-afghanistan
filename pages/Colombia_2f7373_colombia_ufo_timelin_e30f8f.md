## Introduction

[Colombia]({{ 'what-do-colombias-ufo-cases-really-show/' | relative_url }})'s UFO history is less a story of steadily accumulating proof than a record of changing interpretations. The same kinds of unusual lights have been explained very differently over time: as divine signs in the nineteenth century, possible flying saucers during the Cold War, extraterrestrial visitors in the television age, and, more recently, as smartphone videos competing with explanations involving drones, balloons, atmospheric effects and digital misinformation. The timeline reveals not a growing body of confirmed evidence for extraordinary craft, but an evolving relationship between technology, popular culture and public expectations.

One of the clearest examples comes from the earliest widely discussed Colombian sky event. A spectacular display recorded in Montería during 1859 was long remembered as an extraordinary celestial phenomenon, yet modern astronomical research has identified it as an exceptionally low-latitude aurora produced by the Carrington solar storm. ## The 1859 Montería sky display

The earliest major event often included in Colombian UFO timelines did not originally involve unidentified flying objects at all. Historical records describe residents of Montería witnessing blazing lights, fiery globes and an immense S-shaped feature stretching across the pre-dawn sky. The spectacle frightened observers, many of whom interpreted it through a religious lens, gathering in churches to pray because they believed it signalled divine judgement or catastrophe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0273117715006109" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The grand aurorae borealis seen in Colombia in 1859 - ScienceDirectJanuary 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2016</span></span></span>

More than 150 years later, researchers from the Universidad Nacional de Colombia and collaborators examined archival documents and compared the descriptions with known characteristics of the 1859 Carrington Event—the most powerful geomagnetic storm recorded in modern history. They concluded that the account closely matches an auroral display seen at an exceptionally low latitude, made possible by the unusual intensity of the solar storm and the position of Earth's magnetic field at the time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0273117715006109" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The grand aurorae borealis seen in Colombia in 1859 - ScienceDirectJanuary 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2016</span></span></span>

This episode is historically important because it demonstrates how extraordinary sky phenomena can receive radically different explanations as scientific understanding advances. What once appeared supernatural is now regarded as one of the world's most valuable historical observations of an equatorial aurora.

## The rise of flying-saucer culture

Before the late 1940s, unusual lights reported in Colombia were generally interpreted through religious belief, folklore or natural philosophy. The international "flying saucer" wave changed that framework.

As newspaper reports from the United States spread across Latin America, Colombian journalists and readers adopted a new vocabulary. Strange lights were increasingly described as discs, spacecraft or visitors from other worlds rather than celestial omens. Imported books, magazine articles, radio programmes and later television documentaries reinforced these ideas, making extraterrestrial explanations culturally familiar even when evidence remained limited.

Many mid-twentieth-century Colombian cases survive only as newspaper summaries or later retellings. Because original witness interviews, photographs, meteorological records and aviation data are often unavailable, modern investigators cannot reliably reconstruct many of these reports. Consequently, numerous incidents remain historically interesting but evidentially weak.

The chronology therefore reflects changing cultural interpretation at least as much as changing aerial phenomena.

## Regional hotspots and the power of reputation

By the 1980s and especially the 1990s, Colombian UFO stories increasingly centred on recurring locations rather than isolated national events.

Among the best known is Peña de [Juaica]({{ 'juaica/' | relative_url }}) in Cundinamarca. Reports of mysterious lights near the mountain became widely circulated through television, newspapers and tourism. Indigenous traditions associated with the landscape, together with repeated media coverage, helped establish Juaica as one of Colombia's recognised UFO destinations.

This illustrates an important feature of Colombia's timeline. Once a location develops a reputation for unexplained phenomena, several reinforcing effects often follow:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* more people deliberately watch the sky there;
* visitors arrive already expecting unusual events;
* ambiguous lights receive greater attention than they would elsewhere;
* new reports reinforce the area's existing reputation.

</div>

That process does not prove the observations are mistaken, but it means repeated reports alone cannot demonstrate that a location is objectively more anomalous than others.

## From newspaper reports to viral videos

The largest change in Colombia's UFO chronology occurred with digital cameras and smartphones.

Instead of relying on written witness testimony, modern reports increasingly consist of short videos shared through social media. Cities including Bogotá, Medellín and areas around Buga have generated widely circulated recordings showing bright spheres, distant lights or objects whose distance and size cannot be determined.

Paradoxically, more recordings have not necessarily produced stronger evidence.

Most viral clips lack the information investigators need to identify an object confidently, including:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* accurate location and viewing direction;
* precise date and time;
* multiple camera angles;
* radar or air traffic data;
* calibrated measurements of distance or speed.

</div>

Without these details, even apparently unusual movement may result from camera zoom, changing perspective, autofocus effects or uncertainty about the object's true distance.

The result is an explosion in the number of reported sightings without a comparable increase in evidential quality.

## Why explanations have changed over time

Looking across more than 160 years, Colombian UFO reports reveal a series of distinct interpretive eras rather than a single continuous mystery.

PeriodTypical explanationMid-1800sReligious signs or extraordinary natural phenomena1950s–1970sFlying saucers influenced by global UFO culture1980s–2000sExtraterrestrial visitation, reinforced by television and local hotspot traditionsSmartphone eraMixed explanations involving drones, balloons, aircraft, atmospheric phenomena, digital manipulation and, for some observers, unidentified craft

Scientific knowledge has expanded at the same time. Better understanding of atmospheric optics, astronomy, satellite observations and aerospace technology has provided ordinary explanations for many phenomena that earlier generations found mysterious.

Equally important, investigators now place greater emphasis on corroborating evidence than on witness testimony alone.

## What the timeline actually shows

Colombia's UFO chronology is valuable not because it documents a progression towards confirmed extraterrestrial encounters, but because it illustrates how public interpretations evolve alongside science and communication technology.

The 1859 Montería display stands as the clearest example. A phenomenon once regarded as astonishing and unexplained has become an important historical record of extreme space weather. Later decades demonstrate a different process: media narratives increasingly shaped how people interpreted ambiguous lights, while digital technology greatly increased the visibility of reports without necessarily increasing their reliability.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0273117715006109" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[sciencedirect.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The grand aurorae borealis seen in Colombia in 1859 - ScienceDirectJanuary 1, 2016...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 1, 2016</span></span></span>

Taken together, Colombia's timeline shows that the country's UFO story is best understood as a history of changing explanations. Confirmed natural events, unresolved observations, local folklore and viral online videos all occupy different places within that history, and distinguishing between them is essential for understanding how Colombian UFO culture has developed over time.
