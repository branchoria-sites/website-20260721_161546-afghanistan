## The Dulali “floating city” case

Dulali remains Nigeria’s most interesting UFO report because it was presented as a collective observation rather than a single ambiguous photograph. Journalistic accounts named adult and child witnesses, the village’s chief imam and a local political figure. Witnesses broadly agreed that a luminous or transparent mass moved at low altitude and contained a scene resembling an urban settlement. A local-government chairman said that he had heard about the incident and had considered an investigation, but that serious security problems in the area took priority.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://allafrica.com/stories/201204250260.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: allafrica.com">[allAfrica.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">allafrica.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nigeria: A Visit of the Unknown25 Apr 2012 — Interestingly, the UFO sighting in Dulali has revealed to us as researchers the influence of...</span></span></span>

The alleged number of observers strengthens the case only in a limited sense. Multiple witnesses can establish that a conspicuous stimulus was present, but they do not automatically establish its size, height or physical nature. People viewing the same unfamiliar cloud, reflection or optical effect can influence one another’s descriptions, especially after discussing what they saw. The surviving accounts do not preserve independent statements taken before witnesses had compared impressions.

The case also lacks the [records]({{ 'records-ec9535/' | relative_url }}) needed for reliable reconstruction. No original photographs, video, radar plots, meteorological measurements, flight records or physical traces are publicly associated with it. The precise date is usually given only as March 2011, and the claimed second appearance is not accompanied by a separate evidential record. Most later online versions reproduce substantially the same narrative, sometimes years after the supposed event, rather than providing additional independent reporting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://x.com/Ivory1957/status/1858410281514283444" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: x.com">[x.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">x.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">X (formerly Twitter)A Floating City in Nigeria: The Dulali UFO Encounter...In March 2011, the small village of Dulali in Bauchi State, N...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 2011</span></span></span>

A mirage or unusual cloud display is a plausible category of explanation, particularly because witnesses compared the phenomenon to both a cloud and a city. Complex atmospheric refraction can distort distant landscapes and produce elevated, layered or apparently floating images. Yet applying that explanation specifically to Dulali would require weather conditions, viewing directions and a possible distant source landscape that have not been documented. Calling the event a mirage is therefore a reasonable hypothesis, not a demonstrated solution.

Nor does the account establish an engineered craft. Reports of visible roads and buildings are more consistent with the witnesses’ interpretation of a complex image than with conventional descriptions of a discrete vehicle. The detail about the form passing “behind” or “through” a tree is particularly difficult to evaluate without knowing the observer’s position and the transparency, distance or boundaries of the stimulus. The evidence supports an unusual communal sighting; it does not support confident claims about extraterrestrial technology.

## Claims, explanations and evidence in Nigeria's UFO record
A useful assessment separates the existence of a report from the truth of its most dramatic interpretation.

### Confirmed events

Several basic events are well supported. Residents were interviewed about the Dulali observation; the Lagos alien imagery was deliberately distributed; the false Katsina video circulated widely; NAMA issued an explanation concerning the Abuja airspace incident; and an airline crew recorded lights while travelling towards Nigeria. These are confirmed media or reporting events, not confirmed alien encounters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nairaland.com/2316305/floating-city-witnessed-hundred-villagers" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nairaland.com">[nairaland.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nairaland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Floating City Witnessed By Hundred Of Villagers In Bauchi...15 May 2015 — Pangea: One day I saw an UFO, Carrying garri IFO, It...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2015</span></span></span>

### Contested or unresolved observations

The Dulali phenomenon remains unresolved because the available evidence is too poor to distinguish reliably among atmospheric optics, cloud structure, mistaken distance, embellishment and other possibilities. The 2024 cockpit lights are similarly unresolved at the level of the public video. Apparent movement in night-sky footage can be produced by camera motion, aircraft banking, autofocus, digital stabilisation, atmospheric scintillation or the changing geometry of distant lights. Without reference stars, precise timestamps, aircraft attitude and two independent viewing positions, extraordinary acceleration cannot be measured from the clip alone.

The crew’s statement that the lights were absent from radar does not establish that they were exotic. Aircraft weather radar is designed primarily to detect precipitation and hazardous weather, not every distant light, satellite or small object. A light can therefore be visible without producing a meaningful return on the radar display available to the pilots. The captain’s testimony is relevant evidence of what the crew perceived, but it cannot supply the missing distance and motion measurements.

## Why Nigeria’s evidence base is so thin

No publicly accessible Nigerian equivalent of a dedicated national UFO archive was identified in the available official material. Nigerian aviation bodies investigate safety incidents and manage aircraft movements, but their public-facing functions are not organised around the collection of civilian UFO reports. The Nigerian Safety Investigation Bureau describes its mandate in terms of investigating transport accidents and serious incidents, while NAMA provides air-navigation services and manages controlled airspace.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nsib.gov.ng/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nsib.gov.ng">[Nigerian Safety Investigation Bureau]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nsib.gov.ng</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nsib.gov.ng.</span></span></span>

This institutional structure creates an archival gap. A strange light that does not endanger an aircraft, violate controlled airspace or cause an accident may never enter an official reporting system. The Dulali witnesses reportedly saw no reason to contact authorities because they perceived the event as harmless or spiritually meaningful. Consequently, a potentially investigable observation survived mainly as a newspaper feature rather than as a time-stamped technical file.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nairaland.com/2316305/floating-city-witnessed-hundred-villagers" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nairaland.com">[Nairaland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nairaland.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Floating City Witnessed By Hundred Of Villagers In Bauchi...15 May 2015 — Pangea: One day I saw an UFO, Carrying garri IFO, It...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 2015</span></span></span>

International civilian databases cannot fully repair that gap. Systems operated by organisations such as the Mutual UFO Network depend on self-reporting, internet access, awareness of the organisation and volunteer investigation. MUFON describes a worldwide reporting and chapter structure, but its public chapter material does not demonstrate a robust, transparent Nigerian case programme. Such databases can preserve leads, yet they are not representative surveys of what populations see.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://mufon.com/find-a-chapter/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: mufon.com">[MUFON]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">mufon.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on mufon.com.</span></span></span>

The Katsina and Lagos cases spread because imagery travelled rapidly and invited emotional interpretation before its source was established. ## What would make a Nigerian case persuasive?

A stronger Nigerian UFO investigation would begin by treating “unidentified” as a temporary status rather than a conclusion. Researchers would need the original file rather than a social-media copy, the precise time and location, the direction of view, weather conditions, aircraft and satellite activity, witness positions and any relevant radar or air-traffic information.

For events involving alleged rapid movement, one video is rarely sufficient. A scientific observation programme would use synchronised cameras at separated locations, allowing distance and trajectory to be calculated by triangulation. Useful systems would also combine wide-field and close-up imaging with radio, acoustic, environmental and radar measurements. Multimodal observation is important because it helps distinguish a physical object from camera artefacts, reflections and atmospheric effects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18566" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

Witness testimony would remain valuable, especially in a case such as Dulali, but interviews should be conducted individually and promptly. Investigators should record witnesses’ exact words before proposing labels such as spacecraft, spirit, mirage or drone. Reports should distinguish what was directly perceived—light, shape, sound, duration and direction—from later interpretations.

On the available public evidence, Nigeria has no confirmed case of an extraterrestrial vehicle and no documented national pattern of technologically extraordinary aerial objects. It does have genuine reports of unfamiliar phenomena, several instructive misidentifications and fabrications, and one notable mass-witness narrative whose evidential potential was largely lost through delayed and incomplete documentation. The enduring lesson of the Nigerian cases is that unexplained does not mean inexplicable, while a weakly documented event may remain unresolved even when its true cause was entirely ordinary.
